TODAY'S FOCUS

Over the last fifty years the world has grown smaller. Oil carried across the sea tied the wealth of rich nations to the politics of the Middle East. New weapons and new networks allowed a small group to strike at a great power. And new ways of sending words and pictures have made every event seem close at hand. This lesson follows these three threads: dependence, grievance, and speed. As you read, ask what each did for free peoples, and what each cost them. This is the question of the week, and of the Quarter 4 Capstone.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Osama bin Laden
oh-SAH-mah bin LAH-den

Osama bin Laden (1957 to 2011) was born in Saudi Arabia to a family that had grown rich in the building trade. He went to Afghanistan in the 1980s to help the Afghan rebels against the Soviet Union, and there he built a network of fighters. After the Gulf War he turned against Saudi Arabia and the United States.

In 1996 and again in 1998 he issued statements that called on his followers to kill Americans. Al-Qaeda carried out a series of attacks that grew larger, until September 11, 2001. For ten years he hid, and in May 2011 American commandos found him in a compound in Abbottabad, Pakistan, and killed him.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
George W. Bush
JORJ DUB-ul-yoo BOOSH

George W. Bush (born 1946) is the son of the forty-first president, George H. W. Bush. He studied at Yale and Harvard, worked in the oil business, and was elected governor of Texas in 1994. He won the presidency in 2000 in a contest decided by the Supreme Court after a close vote in Florida.

The attacks of September 11 came in his first year in office. He declared a war on terror, sent troops to Afghanistan and Iraq, and set up the Department of Homeland Security. His decision to invade Iraq is still argued over. He left office in 2009, in the midst of a deep financial crisis.

THE SCREEN AND THE SPEED OF EVENTS

A third thread is speed. In 1980 an American company began the first television news channel to run twenty-four hours a day. In 1991 the Gulf War was shown live. In 1989 a British scientist at a laboratory in Switzerland, Tim Berners-Lee, proposed what he called the World Wide WebThe World Wide WebThe World Wide Web is the system of linked pages and addresses, built on top of the internet, that lets anyone publish and read information worldwide. Read more below., which turned a network used by scientists into a tool for everyone. By the early 2000s hundreds of millions of people were online. Facebook began in 2004, YouTube in 2005, Twitter in 2006, and in 2007 the iPhone put the internet into a pocket. social mediaSOH-shul MEE-dee-uhWebsites and apps through which people share messages, pictures, and video with many others. See the glossary box. allowed anyone to send words and pictures to thousands at once, without an editor.

The effect was large, and it cut both ways. In December 2010 a Tunisian street vendor set himself on fire in protest against the police. Phone videos of the protests spread across the Arab world, and within weeks the rulers of Tunisia and Egypt had fallen. Many hoped for free government. But in most of these countries the old rulers returned or war followed, and in Syria a civil war killed hundreds of thousands and drove millions into exile. The same tools helped terrorists to spread their message and recruit followers. Rumors now moved faster than facts could be checked. News of a bank failure in New York in September 2008 sent a financial panic around the world within days. A new virus that appeared in one Chinese city in late 2019 had reached every continent within months.

This is part of what is called globalizationgloh-bul-ih-ZAY-shunThe growing connection of the world’s economies and peoples through trade, travel, and communication. See the glossary box.. Trade, travel, and money flow across borders more freely than ever. China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, and its factories became the workshop of the world. Goods became cheaper, and many millions escaped poverty. But the same ties made nations depend on one another, and on distant makers of goods, in ways that a sudden shock could break.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The World Wide Web

The internet is a network that links computers around the world. It began in the late 1960s as a project of the American defense department, and universities soon joined. It was hard to use. In 1989 Tim Berners-Lee, working at the laboratory called CERN, proposed a system of pages joined by links, each with its own address. The first website went online in 1991.

Berners-Lee did not charge for the idea, and in 1993 CERN made the software free for all. Browsers that showed pictures followed, and the web spread quickly. It changed how people learn, shop, work, and argue. It also made it easy to spread false information, and hard to tell what is true.

BEFORE YOU WRITE

You have now seen the way free peoples met claims of right against the state, in South Africa, the American South, and the Balkans, and the pressures of oil, terror, and speed. Does a smaller world make free peoples safer or more exposed? Make two lists. One holds the evidence for safer: shared markets, alliances such as NATO, quick aid for the afflicted, and the pressure of world opinion. The other holds the evidence for more exposed: dependence on oil and distant factories, terror networks, and the speed of rumor and panic. Mark where the two lists overlap, since many things on one belong on the other. Keep all of your notes from this quarter. You will use them for the Quarter 4 Capstone.
