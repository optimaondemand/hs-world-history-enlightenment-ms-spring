TODAY'S FOCUS

What may a people do when its own government wrongs it? And what may other nations do about it? This lesson takes up three cases from the last half of the century: South Africa, where a white minority ruled a black majority by law; the American South, where black citizens met the same kind of law with peaceful protest; and Yugoslavia, where a federation broke apart in war. As you read, notice the arguments each side made, and the means they chose. You will use them for the Quarter 4 Capstone.

SOUTH AFRICA: THE LAW OF APARTHEID

In 1948 white South Africans voted into power the National Party, which promised a system of racial separation, apartheiduh-PAR-taytAn Afrikaans word meaning apartness. It names the system of laws in South Africa that separated the races and gave power to the white minority. See the glossary box.. Over the next years the laws sorted every person by race. They set apart the places where people could live and work, made black South Africans carry passes, and denied them the vote. The African National Congress, founded in 1912, had at first asked for fair treatment by petitions and marches. In 1960, at Sharpeville, police opened fire on a crowd that was protesting the pass laws and killed sixty-nine people. The government banned the ANC.

Its leaders said that they meant to avoid killing people. Mandela was arrested in 1962, and in 1963 he and others were put on trial at Rivonia, charged with sabotage and plots to overthrow the state. He spoke from the dock in April 1964, and you will read part of what he said. The court sentenced him to life in prison. He spent twenty-seven years in prison, eighteen of them on Robben Island, a bleak island off Cape Town.

Resistance never stopped. In 1976 schoolchildren in Soweto marched against being taught in Afrikaans, and police killed hundreds in the riots that followed. The economy slowed, and many white leaders came to think that the system could not last.

In 1989 F. W. de Klerk became president. He lifted the ban on the ANC and released Mandela on February 11, 1990. Years of hard talks followed, with violence breaking out along the way. In April 1994 all South Africans voted in the same election for the first time, and Mandela became president. His government set up a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which let people confess to crimes in return for amnesty. Mandela served one term and stepped down in 1999. Notice what he did not do: he did not take revenge, and he did not cling to power. Aristotle would call that the virtue of magnanimity.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Nelson Mandela
NEL-sun man-DEL-uh

Nelson Mandela (1918 to 2013) was born into a chief’s family in the Eastern Cape and trained as a lawyer. In Johannesburg he and Oliver Tambo opened the first black law firm in the country. He joined the ANC and led its Defiance Campaign of 1952, a peaceful protest against unjust laws.

In prison he read, studied, and won the respect of his guards. After his release he led the talks that ended apartheid. In 1993 he and de Klerk shared the Nobel Peace Prize. As president he worked to hold the nation together, even backing the mostly white rugby team. He lived to be ninety-five.

THE UNITED STATES: THE LETTER FROM BIRMINGHAM

In the American South the law also separated the races, though the methods were not those of Pretoria. After the Supreme Court ruled against school segregation in 1954, the Southern states fought the decision. A year later a Montgomery seamstress, Rosa Parks, was arrested for refusing to give up her seat on a bus. A young minister, Martin Luther King Jr.MAR-tin LOO-ther KING JOON-yerMartin Luther King Jr. was the Baptist minister who led the American civil rights movement by peaceful protest. Read more below., led a boycott of the buses that lasted more than a year and ended in victory.

King’s method was nonviolencenon-VY-uh-lensThe method of seeking change by peaceful means only, even when others use force against you. See the glossary box.. It meant breaking an unjust law in the open, accepting the penalty, and meeting violence with love. He had learned it from the Gospels and from Gandhi. In April 1963 he was jailed in Birmingham, Alabama, and wrote a long letter to white clergymen who had called his protests untimely. He argued that a just law is one that squares with the moral law, and that “an unjust law is no law at all,” in the words of Saint Augustine. He also cited Saint Thomas Aquinas and the Declaration of Independence. His claim was not that the American founding was false, but that America had not yet kept its promise.

In August 1963 about 250,000 people marched on Washington, and King gave his speech about a dream. Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. King was shot in Memphis on April 4, 1968. Compare him with Mandela. Both sought equal rights, and both appealed to a standard above the law of their country. They chose different means because they faced different governments. King spoke to a government that had to answer to voters, and Mandela faced one that did not. Ask whether that was the only difference.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Martin Luther King Jr.
MAR-tin LOO-ther KING JOON-yer

Martin Luther King Jr. (1929 to 1968) was born in Atlanta, the son and grandson of Baptist ministers. He studied theology and earned a doctorate at Boston University. In 1954 he became pastor of a church in Montgomery, Alabama, and a year later he found himself leading the bus boycott.

He founded the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and led campaigns in Albany, Birmingham, and Selma. He won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964, at thirty-five. His speeches, which joined the language of the Bible with that of the Declaration of Independence, are among the best known in American history. His house was bombed, and he was jailed about thirty times.

Politicians looked for support in the old quarrels.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Slobodan Milosevic
sloh-BOH-dan mih-LOH-seh-vich

Slobodan Milosevic (1941 to 2006) was a banker and a Communist official who came to power in Serbia in 1987. At a speech in Kosovo he told Serbs that no one would beat them again, and the crowd made him a hero. He had the Serbian leaders in Kosovo and the other provinces removed, and he took control of the army.

He made no secret of his wish to hold power. His wars in Croatia, Bosnia, and Kosovo cost many thousands of lives and ruined the Serbian economy. He was voted out in 2000 and indicted by the war crimes court at The Hague, which charged him with crimes against humanity and genocide. He was still on trial when he died.

🏯 CITY
Sarajevo

Sarajevo lies in a narrow valley surrounded by hills. Its mosques, churches, and synagogues stood close together, and for centuries its people of different faiths had lived side by side. It hosted the Winter Olympic Games in 1984. In June 1914, the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand on its streets began the First World War.

In April 1992 Serb forces occupied the hills and besieged the city, shelling it daily. About eleven thousand people were killed, and many were shot as they crossed open streets or stood in lines for water. The siege lasted nearly four years, the longest in modern European warfare. The city was rebuilt after 1996.

WHAT TO TAKE AWAY

Make a chart with three columns, one for each case. For each, write who held power, what claim the people made against it, what means they used, and what outside nations did. Then ask what the cases teach about the limits of the state. A republic needs offices whose authority is recognized and laws that treat citizens alike. When a state ignores these, its subjects may claim a right to resist, and the question then is how, and with what cost. Keep your chart. You will need it for the Quarter 4 Capstone.
