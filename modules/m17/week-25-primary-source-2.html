PRIMARY SOURCE 2
The Nuremberg Laws (September 15, 1935)
Two laws that stripped German Jews of citizenship and many other rights.
💡 BEFORE YOU READ

The Reichstag passed these laws in September 1935 at the Nazi Party rally in Nuremberg. The first took away citizenship from Jews. The second banned marriage and other relations between Jews and other Germans. Later decrees spelled out who would count as a Jew.

The words of these laws are hard to read, and they are meant to be. They show how a state can describe cruelty in the language of ordinary statutes. Read them as a historian reads any source. Ask what claim about people the laws make, what rights they remove, and what they tell us about the aims of the regime.

The Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honor
Reichstag, September 15, 1935. English wording follows the version commonly published in translation. [...] marks a cut. Working placeholder: this excerpt was written from memory for the draft and will be replaced with a verified transcription from a cited edition before release.

Reich Citizenship Law. Article 2. (1) A Reich citizen is only that subject of German or kindred blood who, by his conduct, shows that he is both desirous and fit to serve the German people and Reich faithfully. (2) The right to citizenship is acquired by the granting of Reich citizenship papers.

[...]

Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honor. Article 1. (1) Marriages between Jews and citizens of German or kindred blood are forbidden. Marriages concluded in defiance of this law are void, even if concluded abroad to circumvent this law.

Article 2. Extramarital relations between Jews and nationals of German or kindred blood are forbidden.

Article 3. Jews may not employ in their households female subjects of German or kindred blood who are under 45 years old.

📌 A NOTE FOR YOU

Notice the weight that the word “blood” carries here, and notice what the Citizenship Law says about conduct. Ask what these laws say a citizen is, and who decides. Ask also what conduct could change a person’s status under the text, and what could not.
