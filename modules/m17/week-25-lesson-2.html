TODAY'S FOCUS

Hitler became Chancellor of a coalitionkoh-uh-LISH-unA government made up of several parties that agree to govern together. See the glossary box. government that he did not control. In less than two years he was the master of Germany. He did not do it by one great stroke. He did it by a series of steps, almost all of them dressed in the form of law. This lesson follows those steps, from the Reichstag fire to the Nuremberg Laws. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question.

FIRE AND EMERGENCY

Hitler at once called for a new election. On the night of February 27, 1933, the Reichstag building in Berlin burst into flames. Police found a young Dutch Communist named Marinus van der Lubbe inside. Hitler and Hermann Göring told the nation that the fire was the signal for a Communist uprising. Historians have argued for decades about who set the fire. Most now think van der Lubbe acted alone, though the Nazis were quick to use it.

The next day President Hindenburg signed an emergency decree “for the Protection of the People and State.” It suspended the civil libertiesSIV-ul LIB-er-teezFreedoms that the law guarantees to citizens, such as free speech, a free press, and the right to assemble. See the glossary box. that the constitution guaranteed. It ended freedom of speech, of the press, and of assembly, and it allowed police to hold people without charge. In the weeks that followed, thousands of Communists and others were arrested. The Nazi SA, the SturmabteilungSHTOORM-ap-ty-loongThe Nazi Party’s private army of street fighters, called the SA or brownshirts. See the glossary box., was made an auxiliary police. In the election of March 5, the Nazis won 44 percent of the vote. It was not a majority, but together with their nationalist allies they had one.

THE ENABLING ACT

Hitler wanted the power to make laws without the Reichstag. An amendment to the constitution needed a two-thirds vote, and the Nazis did not have it. He solved the problem. The Communist deputies were in prison or in hiding, so they could not vote. On March 23, 1933, the Reichstag met in the Kroll Opera House, because the burned building could not be used. Armed SA men stood in the halls and outside.

Otto WelsOT-oh VELSOtto Wels was the leader of the Social Democratic Party who spoke against the Enabling Act in the Reichstag in March 1933. Read more below., the leader of the Social Democrats, rose to speak against the bill. He said that they could take away the freedom and lives of his party, but not its honor. The Center Party, the party of the Catholics, voted yes after Hitler made promises about the Church. The vote was 444 to 94. Only the Social Democrats voted no.

The law was called the Law to Remedy the Distress of People and Reich, and it is known as the Enabling Act. It let Hitler’s cabinet pass laws without the Reichstag for four years, even laws that went against the constitution. You will read it in this week’s first primary source. Notice how the vote itself followed the rules. It was a lawful act that ended the rule of law.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Otto Wels
OT-oh VELS

Otto Wels (1873 to 1939) was a saddle maker who rose in the Social Democratic Party and became its chairman in 1919. He had served in the Reichstag for many years and had spoken for the republic against both the Communists and the extreme right.

On March 23, 1933, with SA men in the hall, Wels rose to oppose the Enabling Act. His party, he said, would stand by the principles of humanity, justice, freedom, and socialism. Hitler answered him with scorn. Wels fled to Prague and then to Paris, where he died in September 1939. His party was the only one to vote no, and its courage is remembered in Germany today.

BRINGING EVERYTHING INTO LINE

With the Enabling Act in hand, the Nazis set out on what they called GleichschaltungGLYKH-shal-toongThe Nazi policy of bringing every part of German life under party control. The word means ‘bringing into line.’ See the glossary box., a word that means “bringing into line.” State governments were put under Nazi control. In April a law dismissed Jews and political enemies from the civil service. In May the labor unions were shut down and their leaders arrested, and their money was seized. On July 14 a law declared that the Nazi Party was the only party allowed in Germany. Books by Jewish and other authors were burned in public squares.

The regime also built an apparatus of fear. The first camp for political prisoners opened at Dachau in March 1933. The Gestapoguh-STAH-pohThe secret state police of Nazi Germany. It could arrest people with no court order. Within a year there was no free press, no free party, and no free union left in Germany. Notice how each step was announced as a response to an emergency.

THE NIGHT OF THE LONG KNIVES

Hitler still faced two dangers. One was the SA, which had grown to about three million men under Ernst Röhm. Röhm wanted the SA to replace the regular army, and the generals were alarmed. The other was the aged Hindenburg, who might die at any time and leave a gap at the top. Hitler needed the army’s support to take the president’s place. He decided to strike.

On June 30, 1934, and in the next two days, Hitler’s black-shirted guards, the SS, arrested and shot Röhm and many other SA leaders. They also murdered old enemies who had nothing to do with the SA, including former Chancellor Schleicher and his wife. The official count was 85 dead, but the real number was probably over a hundred. Hitler told the Reichstag that for that night he had been the supreme judge of the German people. Few Germans protested, and the army thanked him.

Hindenburg died on August 2, 1934. THE NUREMBERG LAWS

The regime always aimed its hatred at the Jews, who numbered about half a million people, less than one in a hundred Germans. Hitler had long made them his scapegoatSKAYP-gohtA person or group that is blamed unfairly for the troubles of others. See the glossary box.. In April 1933 the SA led a boycott of Jewish shops. That month a law pushed Jews out of the civil service. Over the next two years Jews lost their places in many professions and were pushed out of public life.

In September 1935 the Reichstag met in the city of Nuremberg during the great Nazi Party rally and passed two laws. The Reich Citizenship Law said that only a person of “German or kindred blood” could be a full citizen. Jews became mere “subjects” of the state. The second law forbade marriage and other relations between Jews and persons of “German blood” and barred Jews from hiring certain women as household help. A later decree said who counted as a Jew, based on a person’s grandparents, not on his own beliefs.

These laws made persecutionper-sih-KYOO-shunCruel treatment of people because of their race, religion, or beliefs. See the glossary box. a matter of law. Many Germans joined in, many looked away, and a few helped their neighbors at great risk. The laws also showed the extent of what the Enabling Act had made possible. The state could now take away a person’s citizenship by statute, and the demagogueDEM-uh-gahgA leader who wins power by stirring up the fears and hatreds of the crowd. See the glossary box. of the beer halls had become the master of the law itself.

🏯 CITY
Nuremberg

Nuremberg is a medieval city in Bavaria with a great castle and high walls. In the Middle Ages it was a rich trading town and the home of the painter Albrecht Dürer. Many Germans saw it as a symbol of the old German Empire.

The Nazis held their party rallies there each year from 1933. They built a vast rally ground outside the city where hundreds of thousands of marchers stood in ranks under searchlights and banners. The rallies were designed to make the individual feel a part of something huge. In September 1935 the Reichstag was called there to pass the racial laws. After the war the city was chosen for the trials of the Nazi leaders, in 1945 and 1946.

LAW WITHOUT LIBERTY

Why did so many Germans accept this? Part of the answer is fear. The Gestapo, the camps, and the SA taught people to keep quiet. Part of it is success. By 1936 the number of unemployed had fallen from six million to well under two million, partly because the government spent heavily on roads and on weapons. Hitler also won back lands and rights that Versailles had taken. Many Germans cared more about jobs and national pride than about the freedoms they had lost.

A republic does not just have laws. It has laws that bind the men who rule. The Nazi state kept the forms of law and removed the substance. A decree, a vote, a statute, each one gave a legal cover to an act that destroyed the limits on power. In the next weeks the course turns to Asia, where another military state was rising. Then it returns to Hitler and the road to war.
