TODAY'S FOCUS

On April 2, 1917, President Woodrow Wilson asked Congress to declare war on Germany. Two weeks earlier the tsar of Russia had lost his throne. Within a year Russia would be out of the war and America would be in it. This week asks which of those two events changed the war more. Today’s lesson covers how the United States was drawn in. The next lesson covers Russia’s exit. Keep both in view.

A PRESIDENT WHO WANTED PEACE

Wilson had spent his career as a professor and a university president. He won the presidency in 1912, and he won again in 1916 with a slogan that said he had kept the country out of war. For two and a half years he held to neutrality. He protested the sinking of the Lusitania, and he protested the British blockade too.

Americans were divided. Many had German or Irish roots and felt no love for Britain. Others felt close to Britain and France by language, law, and trade. American banks had lent the Allies billions of dollars, and American factories sold them shells. But most Americans saw no reason to send their sons to Europe.

Wilson also tried to act as a peacemaker. In December 1916 he asked both sides to state their war aims. Germany answered vaguely, and the Allies replied with demands that no peace conference could easily meet. The fighting went on.

In January 1917 Wilson offered a plan. He told the Senate that the war should end in “a peace without victory.” Victory, he said, would mean a peace forced on the loser, and such a peace would not last. It was a lofty hope, and it was a risky one, because it asked proud nations to accept an end without a winner. Germany answered it within days.

TWO MISTAKES IN BERLIN

On January 31 Germany announced that its U-boatsYOO-bohtShort for the German word Unterseeboot, meaning ‘undersea boat.’ It is the name for a German submarine. See the glossary box. would sink all ships near Britain without warning. Wilson broke off diplomatic relations on February 3, but he still hoped to avoid war.

Then came a second blow. In January the German foreign secretary, Arthur Zimmermann, had sent a secret message to Germany’s diplomats in Mexico. It said that if the United States entered the war, Germany should offer Mexico an alliance. In return, Germany would help Mexico to win back Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. The message was written in a cipherSY-ferA system of secret writing used to hide the meaning of a message. See the glossary box., and Zimmermann thought it was safe.

It was not. British codebreakers in a naval office called Room 40 had broken the German code. They read the message and kept it secret for weeks, until they found a way to show it to the Americans without revealing how they had read it. Wilson received the text on February 24, and newspapers printed it on March 1. Many Americans first thought it was a British forgery. Then Zimmermann confirmed it himself in public. That admission did more to turn American opinion against Germany than anything a British writer could have said.

The offer was not realistic. Mexico was torn by its own revolution and had no hope of conquering the American Southwest. Mexico’s government declined. But Americans in the West and South, who had cared little about the war, now saw danger on their own border.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Arthur Zimmermann
AR-toor TSIM-er-mahn

Arthur Zimmermann (1864 to 1940) was a German diplomat who became the foreign secretary in November 1916. He was one of the few top officials who did not come from the old noble families, and he had a reputation for being blunt.

In January 1917 he supported the decision to resume unrestricted submarine warfare. He also sent the telegram that bears his name, offering Mexico an alliance against the United States. When the British gave the text to Wilson, many people could not believe a government would do something so reckless.

Then Zimmermann told journalists in March that the message was real. His statement ended any doubt in America. He left office in August 1917.

THE DECISION

In mid-March the news from Russia changed the picture. Tsar Nicholas II gave up his throne on March 15. Until then, some Americans had hesitated to fight beside an autocracyaw-TOK-ruh-seeGovernment by one ruler who holds unlimited power. See the glossary box.. Now the Allies could be described as a league of free peoples. In the same week, German submarines sank three American merchant ships. Wilson’s cabinet voted for war.

On the evening of April 2 Wilson spoke to a packed Congress. Germany’s submarine war, he said, was “a war against all nations.” He argued that America must fight, not for conquest, but for the rights of free peoples. “The world must be made safe for democracy,” he said. You will read part of this speech as your first primary source this week.

The Senate voted for war on April 4 by 82 to 6. The House voted on April 6 by 373 to 50. Wilson was careful to call the United States an “associated power,” not an ally. The nation did not sign the secret treaties that the European Allies had made among themselves. This choice would matter at the peace conference.

Notice that Wilson spoke of ideals, but other forces were at work too. America had money tied up in an Allied victory, its ships were being sunk, and its honor was challenged. Real events, not speeches alone, had pushed the country toward war. A good historian keeps both kinds of cause in view.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Woodrow Wilson
WOOD-roh WIL-sun

Woodrow Wilson (1856 to 1924) was the twenty-eighth president of the United States. He had been a professor of politics and the president of Princeton University before he became governor of New Jersey and then president in 1913. He was a serious man with strong moral beliefs, raised in a Presbyterian family.

Wilson kept the United States neutral for nearly three years. When he finally asked for war in April 1917, he cast it as a crusade for democracy. He spoke with great skill, but critics said that he mixed moral language with ordinary power politics.

At the end of the war he traveled to Paris, the first sitting president to cross the Atlantic, to help make the peace. You will read about his hopes for the peace, and the Senate’s response, in a later lesson. He suffered a severe stroke in 1919 and left office in 1921.

WHAT AMERICA COULD DO

America’s army in April 1917 was small. It had about 130,000 regular soldiers, plus the state National Guard. It had almost no tanks, few modern guns, and no experience of this kind of war. In May Congress passed a law to draft men, and the army began to grow. General John Pershing took command of the American Expeditionary Force. He insisted that Americans fight as a separate army under their own flag, rather than serving in British and French units. British and French leaders wanted men at once and argued with him.

American help came quickly in other forms. American destroyers joined the convoys that protected ships from U-boats. American banks kept lending money to the Allies, and American farms and factories fed and armed them. At home, the government took charge of the economy. A new Food Administration under Herbert Hoover urged families to save wheat and meat for the army and the Allies. The Treasury sold Liberty Bonds to ordinary citizens, and the sales raised about seventeen billion dollars over the course of the war. The home front you studied last week now had an American version. In the first year, the biggest contribution was hope. The Allies were tired and near the edge, and a rich new ally promised that time was on their side.

But time was needed. By the end of 1917 only a few American divisions were in France. A year after the war message, the Allies still waited for the mass of American soldiers. In the meantime, a very different event was taking place in the east.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Room 40 and the Cipher War

Room 40 was a secret office of the British Admiralty, set up in the autumn of 1914. Its workers were not sailors. They were professors, linguists, and puzzle experts who worked to break German codes. The name came from the number of the room in the Admiralty building.

The Germans sent many messages by radio and by cable, using ciphers that replaced words and letters with numbers. Room 40 collected German codebooks and slowly learned to read the messages. Britain also cut Germany’s overseas telegraph cables early in the war, which forced German diplomats to send messages through other countries’ lines.

In January 1917 Room 40 read Zimmermann’s telegram. The British kept it quiet until they could use it safely. The episode shows how information, as well as armies, could change the course of a war.
