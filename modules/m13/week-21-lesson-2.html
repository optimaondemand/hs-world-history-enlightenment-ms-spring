TODAY'S FOCUS

While America was entering the war, Russia was leaving it. This lesson follows two revolutions in 1917, the rise of Vladimir Lenin, the harsh peace that Germany forced on Russia, and the civil war that followed. It also puts a hard question before you. Lenin took power by promising peace, and the peace he signed was a very costly one.

THE TSAR FALLS

By 1917 Russia was exhausted. Its armies had lost millions of men. Its railways were breaking down, and food was short in the cities. Tsar Nicholas II had taken personal command of the army in 1915 and left the government at home in confusing hands. In March, bread riots broke out in the capital, Petrograd, on the day now celebrated as International Women’s Day. Strikes spread, and the soldiers who were sent to restore order joined the crowds. Nicholas gave up his throne on March 15, and the three-hundred-year rule of the Romanov family ended.

A note on dates: Russia then used an older calendar that ran thirteen days behind the one used in the West. Russians called these events the February Revolution. In this course we give the Western dates.

Two powers now shared Russia. One was a Provisional Government, made up of liberal politicians who hoped to build a lawful republic. The other was the Petrograd sovietSOH-vee-etThe Russian word for council. In 1917 soviets were councils of workers, soldiers, or peasants. See the glossary box., a council of workers and soldiers that held real power in the streets. The Provisional Government kept the war going out of loyalty to the Allies. In the summer its last great attack failed, and the army began to melt away. Soldiers deserted by the thousands. Peasants seized the landlords’ fields.

🏯 CITY
Petrograd

Petrograd was the Russian capital in 1917. Peter the Great founded the city in 1703 as St. Petersburg, and he meant it to be Russia’s “window on the West.” Its broad avenues, canals, and grand palaces were built to rival the capitals of Europe. In 1914, when Russia went to war with Germany, the city was renamed Petrograd to sound less German.

The Winter Palace, the tsars’ great residence, stood beside the Neva River. Factories ringed the city, and their workers were poorly paid and angry by 1917. Both the March and the November revolutions began here. After Lenin’s death in 1924 the city was renamed Leningrad. In 1991 it again became St. Petersburg.

LENIN

Vladimir Lenin did not seem likely to rule Russia. He had spent years in exile, and in early 1917 he was living in Switzerland. His Bolshevik party was small. But the German government saw a use for him. It allowed him and a few companions to cross Germany in a sealed train, hoping he would stir up trouble for Russia’s war effort. Leninvlah-DEE-meer LEN-inVladimir Lenin was the Bolshevik leader who seized power in Russia in November 1917. Read more below. arrived in Petrograd in April and announced a program, which you will read in your second primary source. He called for no support for the Provisional Government, an end to the war, and all power to the soviets. His slogan was “Peace, Land, and Bread.”

Many Bolsheviks thought he was going too far. But Lenin’s will was strong. He pressed his party step by step. In the autumn the Bolsheviks won a majority in the Petrograd soviet. On November 7 they seized government buildings and arrested the ministers of the Provisional Government. There was little fighting. Some leading Bolsheviks had opposed the plan, and some even warned the press. It was a coup by a small, determined group, and it succeeded partly because no one was ready to resist it.

The new rulers did not rest. In elections for a Constituent Assembly, called to write a constitution, the Bolsheviks won about a quarter of the votes. A different party, the Socialist Revolutionaries, won the most. The Assembly met in January 1918. Bolshevik soldiers sent it home after a single day. A republic that rested on voting had been replaced by a regime that rested on force.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Vladimir Lenin
vlah-DEE-meer LEN-in

Vladimir Ilyich Lenin (1870 to 1924) was born Vladimir Ulyanov. He studied law, but he became a professional revolutionary after his older brother was hanged for a plot against the tsar. He led the Bolshevik party from 1903.

Lenin believed that a small, disciplined party could lead the working class to power. He was a gifted writer and an uncompromising leader. He pressed his party to seize power in 1917, and he insisted on signing a harsh peace with Germany when others wanted to fight on. His government created a secret police, the Cheka, in December 1917.

Lenin suffered strokes beginning in 1922 and died in January 1924. He had built a state that used terror as a tool, and his successor Stalin would turn that tool on his own party.

PEACE AT A PRICE

Lenin kept his promise of peace, but not on easy terms. A cease-fire, or armisticeAR-muh-stisAn agreement to stop fighting. It is not yet a peace treaty. See the glossary box., was signed in December 1917. Peace talks began at the town of Brest-Litovsk. The Germans demanded huge areas of land, and the Bolshevik delegation, led by Leon Trotsky, tried to stall. In February Trotsky announced “no war, no peace,” meaning that Russia would stop fighting but would not sign. Germany answered by marching forward again, covering many miles in days. Lenin then told his party they must sign or lose everything. Some of his closest colleagues disagreed. He won the vote.

The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, signed in March 1918, took from Russia Poland, the Baltic lands, and Ukraine, along with Finland’s independence. Russia lost about a third of its people and much of its coal, iron, and farmland. Germany’s gains were a form of annexationan-ek-SAY-shunTaking over another country’s land and making it part of your own. See the glossary box. in all but name. It was exactly the kind of victor’s peace that Wilson’s speeches said the world should never impose. It showed what Germany might have demanded of the West if it had won.

Yet Germany did not gain as much as it hoped. It had to keep hundreds of thousands of soldiers in the east to hold its new lands. And the treaty was canceled in November 1918, when Germany lost the war.

CIVIL WAR AND THE COST

Peace with Germany did not bring peace to Russia. The Bolsheviks, called the Reds, faced a civil war against many enemies, called the Whites. The Whites were a mix of officers, landowners, liberals, and others who agreed only that they hated the Bolsheviks. Britain, France, Japan, and the United States sent troops to a few ports, partly to guard supplies and partly to help the Whites. In July 1918 the Bolsheviks killed Nicholas II and his family in the Ural city of Yekaterinburg.

The Reds won by 1920 for several reasons. They held the center of the country and its railways. Trotsky built a disciplined Red Army. And the Whites were divided and often cruel to the peasants whose help they needed. The Bolsheviks used terror too, and the secret police shot or imprisoned thousands. Millions of Russians died in the fighting, and in the famines and diseases that followed it.

Look at the chain of events. A tired tsar fell. A weak government kept an unpopular war going. A determined man, carried home by his country’s enemy, seized the moment. The outcome was not fated. Different choices by the Provisional Government, or by Germany, might have given Russia a different future.
