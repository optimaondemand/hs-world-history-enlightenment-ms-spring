TODAY'S FOCUS

Between 1933 and 1938 Hitler broke the Treaty of Versailles again and again, and each time the great powers let him. Then he demanded part of a free country, Czechoslovakia, and the leaders of Britain and France agreed to give it to him. This lesson follows those steps from the Rhineland to Munich. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: did Munich buy time or cost it?

BREAKING THE CHAINS

In October 1933 Hitler pulled Germany out of the League of Nations and out of a conference on disarmament. In January 1935 the people of the Saar, a coal region that the League had governed since Versailles, voted in a plebiscitePLEB-ih-sytA direct vote by the people of a region or country on a single question, such as which country they wish to belong to. See the glossary box., a direct vote of the people on a single question. Over ninety percent chose to return to Germany. In March 1935 Hitler announced that Germany would have an air force and a draft army. Both were banned by the treaty. Britain, France, and Italy met at Stresa in April to protest, but they did nothing more. Two months later Britain made a naval agreement with Germany, which let Hitler build a fleet in a fixed ratio to Britain’s. France felt betrayed.

The great powers were weak for different reasons. Britain and France were still tired from the First World War, and many people believed that the harshness of Versailles was to blame for Germany’s anger. They were also in the Depression. The United States had turned away from Europe. The Soviet Union was far away and suspected by all.

ETHIOPIA

In October 1935 Mussolini sent an army into Ethiopia, one of the few African states that was still free. The League of Nations voted to punish Italy with sanctions, which are penalties meant to force a country to change its ways. But it did not include oil, coal, or steel, and Britain would not close the Suez Canal. In December the British and French foreign ministers secretly agreed to give Mussolini most of Ethiopia. When the plan was leaked, there was an outcry, and it was dropped. But Italy won the war in May 1936. The League had lost its credibility, and Mussolini, angry at Britain and France, moved closer to Hitler.

THE RHINELAND

Under the Treaty of Versailles and the Locarno agreements of 1925, the German land west of the Rhine River was a demilitarized zonedee-MIL-ih-tuh-ryzd ZOHNAn area where an agreement forbids armies, forts, or weapons. See the glossary box.. No German troops could be stationed there. This protected France. On March 7, 1936, Hitler sent a small force into the zone. About twenty-two thousand men crossed, but only three battalions went to the river itself.

It was a gamble. Hitler’s generals were nervous, and they had orders to withdraw if the French fought. Hitler said later that the forty-eight hours after the march were the most nerve-racking of his life. But France, in the middle of an election, would not move without Britain, and Britain would not. Some British leaders said that the Germans were only walking into their own back garden. Each success raised Hitler’s standing with the Germans and with his own generals. Each also taught him that threats worked.

AXIS AND ARMS

In July 1936 a civil war began in Spain, and Hitler and Mussolini sent planes, tanks, and men to help General Franco. In April 1937 German bombers destroyed the Basque town of Guernica. The Soviet Union sent help to the other side. Spain became a testing ground for new weapons. In October 1936 Germany and Italy announced an agreement that Mussolini called the Axis, and a month later Germany and Japan signed the Anti-Comintern Pact.

On November 5, 1937, Hitler met with his top generals and ministers. An officer named Hossbach took notes. Hitler told them that Germany needed LebensraumLAY-benz-rowmA German word for living space. Hitler used it to mean land in eastern Europe that Germany should take for its own people. See the glossary box., or “living space,” and that he intended to take Austria and Czechoslovakia, perhaps by 1943. Several of the men present objected that Britain and France would fight. Within months Hitler had removed the war minister and the army commander and taken direct control of the armed forces.

THE ANSCHLUSS

In March 1938 Hitler turned on his native Austria. The treaties of 1919 forbade the union of Austria and Germany, which was called the AnschlussAHN-shloosA German word for union. It names the joining of Austria to Germany in 1938. See the glossary box.. Austria’s Chancellor, Kurt Schuschnigg, announced a vote in which the people would choose whether to stay free. Hitler called it a trick and demanded that it be called off. Schuschnigg gave way, and the Austrian Nazi Seyss-Inquart became Chancellor and invited in German troops. On March 12 they crossed the border. Crowds in Vienna cheered. Hitler drove through Austria in triumph, and in a rigged vote in April, about ninety-nine percent were said to approve. Jews and political opponents were arrested within days.

Notice what Hitler gained. Austria’s gold, its army, and its location, which now surrounded Czechoslovakia on three sides. Notice also what the great powers did, which was to protest and to do nothing.

THE SUDETEN CRISIS

Czechoslovakia was a democratic country with a strong army, a mountain line of forts, and one of the finest arms works in Europe, the Skoda factory. It had alliances with France and the Soviet Union. About three million of its citizens were Germans who lived in the border mountains known as the Sudetenlandsoo-DAY-ten-landThe border mountains of Czechoslovakia where about three million ethnic Germans lived. See the glossary box.. A Sudeten German party led by Konrad Henlein, secretly paid by Berlin, demanded self-rule, and Hitler spoke of the right of the Germans to join the Reich.

Neville ChamberlainCHAYM-ber-linNeville Chamberlain was the British Prime Minister from 1937 to 1940 whose policy of appeasement is tied to the Munich Agreement. Read more below., the British Prime Minister, was a sincere man who hated war. He believed that the Germans had real grievances, and that sensible men could settle them by talk. This policy is called appeasementuh-PEEZ-mentThe policy of giving way to a rival’s demands in order to avoid a conflict. See the glossary box.. It meant giving way to a rival’s demands in order to keep the peace. Britain was also not ready. Its air force was small, its people dreaded another war, and its dominions overseas did not want to fight for Czechoslovakia.

In September Chamberlain flew to Germany three times. On September 15 and 22 he met Hitler, who kept raising his demands. On September 29 Hitler, Mussolini, Chamberlain, and the French premier Daladier met at a conference in Munich. The Czechs and the Soviets were not invited. The four men agreed that the Sudetenland should be handed to Germany between October 1 and October 10. Czechoslovakia gave way, and with the Sudetenland it lost its forts and a large part of its industry.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Neville Chamberlain
CHAYM-ber-lin

Neville Chamberlain (1869 to 1940) was a businessman from Birmingham who became mayor of that city, then a government minister, and in 1937 Prime Minister. He was hardworking, proud, and honest. He had seen the horror of the First World War, and he was sure that another would end civilization.

He trusted too much in the power of reason with Hitler, and he disliked and distrusted the Soviets. After Hitler took Prague in March 1939, Chamberlain reversed his policy and promised to defend Poland. He had believed to the end that he could avoid it. On September 3, 1939, he announced over the radio that Britain was at war with Germany. He resigned in May 1940 and died that November.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Radar and the Chain Home Stations

Radar uses radio waves that bounce off objects, so that a station can tell the direction and distance of an aircraft many miles away. British scientists worked on it in the 1930s. By 1937 the first stations of a chain along the east and south coasts were working, and the chain was expanded after Munich.

The stations let fighter planes rise just in time to meet raiders, instead of flying patrols all day. Britain was also building new fighters, the Hurricane and the Spitfire, in these years. Defenders of Chamberlain say this equipment helped to save Britain in 1940. Critics ask whether Germany gained more in the same year.

PEACE FOR OUR TIME?

Chamberlain returned to London to cheering crowds. Standing at the airport, he held up a paper that he and Hitler had signed, which you will read this week. That evening he spoke of “peace with honour” and said, “I believe it is peace for our time.” Daladier, flying home to Paris, expected jeers, and he is said to have muttered, “The fools, they are cheering!” Winston Churchill told the House of Commons that Britain had suffered a total and unmitigated defeat.

Think about what each side might say. Chamberlain’s defenders say that Britain gained a year, in which its radar network, its fighter output, and its air defenses grew. His critics say the Czech army, its forts, and its factories were worth more to Hitler than a year was to Britain. Keep both in mind. Next lesson shows what Hitler did with the year.
