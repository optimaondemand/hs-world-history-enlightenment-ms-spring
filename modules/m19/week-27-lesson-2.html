TODAY'S FOCUS

Hitler had told the world at Munich that he had no more demands. Six months later he broke his word. Britain and France then made a promise to Poland, and Hitler and Stalin made a bargain that astonished the world. This lesson follows the road from Prague to the invasion of Poland. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: did Munich buy time or cost it?

PRAGUE AND THE GUARANTEE

On March 15, 1939, German troops marched into Prague. Czechoslovakia broke into pieces. Hitler took the Czech lands of Bohemia and Moravia as a “protectorate,” and Slovakia became a client state under German protection. This was a turning point. At Munich Hitler had said he wanted only Germans in the Reich. Now he had seized millions of Czechs. Many British people, including Chamberlain, decided that Hitler’s word was worthless and that he could not be bought off. The policy of appeasementuh-PEEZ-mentThe policy of giving way to a rival’s demands in order to avoid a conflict. See the glossary box. had collapsed. Hitler had justified the AnschlussAHN-shloosA German word for union. It names the joining of Austria to Germany in 1938. See the glossary box. and the grab of the Sudetenlandsoo-DAY-ten-landThe border mountains of Czechoslovakia where about three million ethnic Germans lived. See the glossary box. as the right of Germans to join the Reich. Prague was different, because its people were Czechs and Slovaks.

On March 31 Britain, and soon France, promised to defend Poland if it was attacked. It was a remarkable pledge. Britain had never before promised to defend the borders of a country in eastern Europe, and it had no way of sending an army there. It was a warning to Hitler that this time Britain might fight. Britain also began a peacetime draft in April.

THE POLISH QUESTION

Hitler’s next target was Poland. The Treaty of Versailles had made Danzig, a port city with a German majority, a free city under the League. It had also given Poland a strip of land, the Polish Corridor, to reach the sea. The Corridor cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany. Hitler demanded that Danzig return to Germany and that Germany be allowed a road across the Corridor. Poland refused, because its leaders knew that a surrender would be only the first step.

They were right. Hitler’s real aim was LebensraumLAY-benz-rowmA German word for living space. Hitler used it to mean land in eastern Europe that Germany should take for its own people. He believed that Britain and France would again hold back. His foreign minister, Joachim von Ribbentrop, agreed. Notice that Hitler spoke of Danzig and of a plebiscitePLEB-ih-sytA direct vote by the people of a region or country on a single question. THE TRIANGLE

Whether Poland could be defended depended on the Soviet Union. If Germany attacked Poland, a Soviet army could in theory fight it. In the spring and summer of 1939 Britain and France held talks with Stalin, but they did not go well. Stalin had been left out of Munich, and he suspected that the Western powers wanted Hitler to fight Russia instead of them. In May he replaced his foreign minister, Maxim Litvinov, a Jew who had worked for an alliance with the West, with Vyacheslav MolotovMOL-uh-tawfVyacheslav Molotov was Stalin’s foreign minister, who signed the Nazi-Soviet Pact with Germany in August 1939. Read more below.. The British and French sent a low-ranking mission by slow boat. And Poland and Romania refused to let Soviet troops cross their land, because they feared that the Red Army would not go home.

Meanwhile Hitler made a quiet offer. A Soviet army was also fighting the Japanese on the border of Mongolia that summer, and Stalin did not want a war on two fronts.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Vyacheslav Molotov
MOL-uh-tawf

Vyacheslav Molotov (1890 to 1986) was an old Bolshevik who joined the party before the revolution. He took the name Molotov, from the Russian word for hammer. He was a loyal, careful, and tireless servant of Stalin, though cold and dull in manner. He became head of the government in 1930.

In May 1939 Stalin made him foreign minister. In August he met Ribbentrop in Moscow and signed the treaty known as the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact. He lived long enough to see the Soviet Union enter a period of reform, and he defended the pact and his other acts under Stalin until he died in 1986. He was a man who served power and did not question it.

THE PACT

On August 23, 1939, Ribbentrop flew to Moscow. That night he and Molotov signed a non-aggression pactnon-uh-GRESH-un PAKTA treaty in which two countries promise not to attack each other. See the glossary box., a promise that neither country will attack the other. The public treaty said that both would stay at peace for ten years. The world was stunned. The two men who had called each other mortal enemies for years were now partners. Communists around the world, who had been told that Hitler was the worst enemy, were thrown into confusion.

A secret protocol, which you will read, divided eastern Europe into two spheres of influenceSFEER uv IN-floo-ensA region where one great power is allowed to act as it likes, with the agreement of other powers. See the glossary box., regions where one great power is allowed to act as it likes. Finland, Estonia, and Latvia fell in the Soviet sphere, and Lithuania in the German one. Poland was to be cut along the line of the Narew, Vistula, and San rivers. The Soviets also claimed Bessarabia. The Soviet Union kept the protocol’s existence a secret for fifty years and admitted to it only in 1989.

Each man had reasons. Hitler wanted to attack Poland without fighting Russia at the same time, and the pact gave him Soviet grain and oil. Stalin believed he was buying time to build up his army, and he gained land and was not left to face Hitler alone. Notice that the bargain made war certain. With it, Hitler thought he was safe from a two-front war.

INVASION

Britain made its pledge to Poland a formal alliance on August 25. Hitler hesitated for a day, then went ahead. On the night of August 31 the SS dressed some prisoners in Polish uniforms and staged a fake attack on a German radio station at Gleiwitz, near the border. On September 1, 1939, at about a quarter to five in the morning, the German battleship Schleswig-Holstein opened fire on a Polish post at Westerplatte, near Danzig, and German troops crossed the border all along the line.

The attack was swift. Armored columns broke through, and bombers destroyed the roads, railways, and cities behind them. The German press called it BlitzkriegBLITS-kreegA German word for lightning war. It names a swift attack by tanks, planes, and motorized troops. See the glossary box., or “lightning war.” Britain and France sent an ultimatumul-tih-MAY-tumA final demand that must be met, or else there will be war or some other harsh step. See the glossary box., a final demand that must be met or war will follow. When it was rejected, they declared war on September 3. But they sent no real help. The French army made a small advance in the Saar and soon went back.

On September 17 the Soviet army invaded eastern Poland, saying it came to protect the Ukrainians and Belarusians. Warsaw surrendered on September 28, and the two attackers divided the country. In the spring of 1940, Soviet secret police shot about twenty-two thousand captured Polish officers and officials, in the forest of Katyn and other places. Moscow blamed the Germans for fifty years.

🛡 BATTLE
Westerplatte

Westerplatte is a narrow strip of land at the mouth of the harbor of Danzig. Under the treaties, Poland was allowed to keep a small guard there, to protect a store of weapons and supplies. In 1939 the garrison numbered about two hundred men under a Polish major.

At dawn on September 1 the German battleship Schleswig-Holstein opened fire on the post at close range, and German soldiers tried to storm it. The Poles held out for seven days against tanks, bombers, and artillery before they surrendered. Their stand was brave but could not change the outcome of the war. It became a symbol of Polish resistance.

COULD THE WAR HAVE BEEN AVOIDED?

Look for the places where history might have changed. A group of German officers had planned to arrest Hitler if his demands over Czechoslovakia led to war. Munich took the ground from under them. If Britain and France had stood firm in 1936, when Hitler sent troops into the demilitarized zonedee-MIL-ih-tuh-ryzd ZOHNAn area where an agreement forbids armies, forts, or weapons. See the glossary box., or in 1938, they would have faced a Czech army that fought behind strong forts, and a Germany that was not yet ready for a long war. On the other hand, they might have fought alone, since the Soviet Union and the United States stood aside. If the Poles had given way, or if Stalin had joined the West, the outcome would have differed.

A republic has the duty to defend itself, as Hegel said, and war tests whether it is strong. The democracies had a duty to resist, but they put it off. Chamberlain was not a coward. He wanted peace for good reasons, and he was prudent. But prudence without courage can fail. Hitler took risks again and again, and each time the risk paid off, until the risks were too large. Notice that war came because of choices made by a few men, in London, Berlin, and Moscow.
