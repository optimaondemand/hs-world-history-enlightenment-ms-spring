TODAY'S FOCUS

With his rivals gone, Stalin set out to remake the Soviet Union. He pushed for fast industry, forced the peasants onto collective farms, and then turned on his own party and army. These policies built factories and tanks. They also led to famine and mass killing. This lesson asks what the Soviet people paid, and why. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question.

THE GREAT TURN

In October 1928 Stalin launched the first five-year planFYV-yeer PLANA government plan that sets goals for every factory and mine for five years. See the glossary box.. The state would set goals for every factory and mine and would decide what to build. Stalin wanted coal, steel, machines, and power stations, and he wanted them at once. He argued that the Soviet Union stood alone among hostile nations. In the speech you will read this week he said that the country was fifty or a hundred years behind the West and had ten years to catch up. In effect, the end of the New Economic PolicyNOO ee-kuh-NOM-ik POL-ih-seeLenin’s policy from 1921 that let peasants sell extra grain and small traders reopen shops. See the glossary box. was the start of a second revolution, this time ordered from above.

Huge new works rose across the country. Whole cities were built from nothing, among them the steel town of Magnitogorsk in the Ural Mountains. Workers lived in tents and barracks and worked in bitter cold. Many were peasants who had left their villages. Many others were prisoners. Output of coal, iron, and steel rose sharply, though many of the statistics were inflated. Shops had little to sell, and food and housing were scarce.

🏯 CITY
Magnitogorsk

Magnitogorsk lies in the southern Ural Mountains, near a hill called Magnetic Mountain that was rich in iron ore. In 1929 the government decided to build a giant steel works there. Its design was based on the American steel works at Gary, Indiana, and American engineers helped with the plans.

Within a few years a town of hundreds of thousands stood on the empty steppe. Thousands of workers were volunteers, but thousands more were peasants forced from their homes or prisoners. The city’s furnaces made steel for the tanks of the Second World War. It showed what the five-year plans could do, and what they cost.

THE WAR ON THE VILLAGE

The cost fell hardest on the peasants. In 1927 and 1928 many of them held back their grain, because the state paid low prices. Stalin decided to end private farming altogether. This was collectivizationkuh-lek-tiv-ih-ZAY-shunStalin’s policy of forcing peasants to give up their land and animals and join large farms run by the state. See the glossary box.. Peasants were ordered to give up their land, tools, and animals and to join huge state-controlled collective farms. Tractors were kept in state machine stations, so that the government held the machines every farm needed.

Stalin called the better-off peasants kulakkoo-LAHKA Russian word for a better-off peasant. Under Stalin, officials used it for any peasant who resisted collective farming. See the glossary box.s, a word that once meant a tight-fisted village moneylender. The label was used loosely for anyone who resisted. In December 1929 Stalin called for “liquidating the kulaks as a class.” In 1930 and 1931 nearly two million people were driven from their homes and sent to Siberia, Kazakhstan, and the far north. Many died on the way. Peasants fought back. They killed their cattle and horses rather than hand them over, and the country lost much of its livestock. In March 1930 Stalin wrote an article blaming local officials who had become “dizzy with success,” and many peasants left the collective farms. But the state soon pressed them back, and by the late 1930s almost all farms were collectives.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Tractor and the Machine Station

Soviet propaganda showed the tractor as the friend of the peasant, a machine that would free him from the plow and the scythe. In truth, there were few tractors in 1930, and many broke down. The state also kept them in machine stations, which lent the machines to the collective farms and took part of the harvest as payment.

That arrangement gave the government a hold on every farm. A village could not plow its fields without the state’s machines. Technology was used here as a means of control as much as a means of growth. Many peasants came to distrust the machine for this reason. They saw it as the tool of the officials who had taken their land, and not as a gift.

FAMINE

The harvest of 1932 was poor, partly because of bad weather and partly because the farms were in chaos. But the government still demanded the same large amount of grain. It sold grain abroad to pay for machines. Local officials seized seed and food from villages that had not met their quotas. In January 1933 Stalin and the PolitburoPOL-it-byoor-ohThe small committee at the top of the Communist Party that made the main decisions. See the glossary box. ordered roadblocks to stop starving peasants from leaving Ukraine and the Kuban region in search of food.

The result was a famine that killed millions in 1932 and 1933. Historians estimate that between five and seven million people died across the Soviet Union. About three million or more of them lived in Ukraine. In Kazakhstan the famine killed a large share of the Kazakh people, who had been forced to give up their herds. Stalin refused to admit that a famine existed, and he refused help.

Historians still argue about what Stalin meant to do. Some say he aimed to break the Ukrainian people. Others say he pressed policies at any cost and let the people starve. No one disputes that his decisions turned a bad harvest into mass death.

THE GREAT TERROR

In December 1934 an assassin killed Sergei Kirov, the popular party chief of Leningrad. Stalin used the murder as an excuse to attack his enemies. In 1936 the first great show trialSHOH TRY-ulA public trial staged for effect, where the verdict is settled in advance. See the glossary box. opened in Moscow. Zinoviev and Kamenev, the old Bolsheviks who had once helped Stalin, confessed to crimes they had not committed and were shot. Bukharin was tried and shot in 1938. The confessions were forced by threats and torture. The trials were staged for the public, and the verdicts were decided before they began.

Soon a wider purgePURJThe forced removal, and often the killing, of people thought to be disloyal to a ruler or party. See the glossary box. began, in which the party removed members who were thought to be disloyal. It struck the army, the factories, and ordinary people. In the summer of 1937 the head of the NKVDen-kah-vay-DAYThe Soviet secret police and internal security service in the 1930s. The letters stand for the People’s Commissariat of Internal Affairs. See the glossary box., Nikolai Yezhov, received orders to arrest tens of thousands of “enemies.” Local officials asked to be allowed to arrest and shoot even more. Stalin and other members of the Politburo signed long lists of names. In June 1937 Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky and other top generals were shot, and thousands of officers followed.

By 1938, according to Soviet records opened after 1991, about one and a half million people had been arrested and nearly 700,000 had been shot. Many more were sent to the GulagGOO-lahgThe Soviet system of forced labor camps. The word comes from the Russian name of the government office that ran them. See the glossary box.. Stalin ended the worst of the terror in late 1938, and Yezhov himself was arrested and shot. Soviet citizens learned to keep silent, even to distrust their friends.

WHAT KIND OF STATE?

Stalin did succeed at some of the tasks that Classical thinkers set for a state. He built an industry that could produce the tanks and guns that later helped to defeat Hitler. He kept the Soviet Union together. But a republic is supposed to solve its first problem, peace and order at home, by laws that protect its citizens. Stalin kept order through terror. In 1936 the state published a new constitution that promised free speech and the secret ballot. In the next two years it arrested more people than ever before.

Notice, too, that none of this was fated. Bukharin had argued for a slower road, in which peasants would grow richer and feed the cities. Stalin chose differently. The ban on factionFAK-shunA group inside a political party that pushes its own plan against the party’s leaders. And the Soviet Union still had no lawful successionsuk-SESH-unThe way a ruler or leader is replaced after he dies or leaves office. See the glossary box., so Stalin held power until he died.
