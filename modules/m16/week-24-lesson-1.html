TODAY'S FOCUS

In January 1924 Vladimir Lenin died, and the state he had built had no lawful way to choose his successor. Within five years a man whom many leaders thought dull and second-rate had pushed aside every rival and become the master of the Soviet Union. This lesson asks how that happened, and how much of it was planned and how much was luck. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: was Stalin Lenin’s heir or his betrayer?

A DYING LEADER AND HIS PARTY

By 1921 the Bolsheviks had won the civil war, but Russia lay in ruins. Farms and factories were wrecked. A famine along the Volga River killed millions. Peasants rose in revolt, and in March 1921 the sailors of the Kronstadt naval base, once heroes of the revolution, rose against the party. The army crushed them. Lenin saw that the country could not go on as it was. He ended the harsh grain seizures of the civil war and began the New Economic PolicyNOO ee-kuh-NOM-ik POL-ih-seeLenin’s policy from 1921 that let peasants sell extra grain and small traders reopen shops, while the state kept control of big industry and banks. But Lenin did not loosen the party’s grip. Lenin’s government had already created a secret police, the Cheka, in December 1917, and it had used terror against its enemies in the civil war. The state had no free press, no rival parties, and no independent courts.

Lenin’s health then failed. A woman named Fanny Kaplan had shot him in 1918, and some bullets stayed in his body. In May 1922 he had the first of several strokes. A third, in March 1923, took away his speech. He died on January 21, 1924, at the age of fifty-three. The party had never needed one, because it had been led by one dominant man and a small circle around him. Whoever won the next round would win by maneuver, not by any rule.

He had led the Red Army to victory and was a brilliant speaker and writer. But many comrades thought him proud and distant, and he had joined the Bolsheviks only in 1917. Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev were old Bolsheviks and close companions of Lenin, though both had opposed the October uprising, and Lenin had not forgotten it. Nikolai Bukharin was the party’s favorite thinker. And there was Joseph StalinJOH-sef STAH-linJoseph Stalin was the General Secretary who outmaneuvered every rival and ruled the Soviet Union from the late 1920s until his death in 1953. Read more below., who struck many as a plain, dull administrator and not a leader at all.

Stalin was born Ioseb Jughashvili in 1878 in Gori, a town in Georgia, then part of the Russian Empire. His mother hoped he would become a priest. He studied at a church school and then at a seminary in Tiflis, but he was expelled. He joined the revolutionary underground, helped organize robberies to raise money for the party, and was arrested and sent into exile several times. After the revolution Lenin made him Commissar for Nationalities. In April 1922 he became General Secretary of the party.

At the time the post looked dull. The General Secretary handled paperwork, meetings, and appointments. But a man who decides who gets which job soon has many friends. Stalin placed loyal men in posts across the country. Those same men later chose the delegates who voted at party congresses.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Joseph Stalin
JOH-sef STAH-lin

Joseph Stalin (1878 to 1953) chose the name Stalin, from the Russian word for steel, as a young revolutionary. He was not a gifted speaker. His strengths were patience, a remarkable memory, hard work, and a gift for knowing what other men wanted and feared. He read the papers that crossed his desk and he missed little.

As General Secretary he built a web of loyal officials, and by 1929 he stood alone at the top. He ruled until he died on March 5, 1953. Ask what Stalin pursued and with what success. He pursued power for himself and a strong industrial state, and he paid for both with the lives of millions of his own people.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Leon Trotsky
TROT-skee

Leon Trotsky (1879 to 1940) was born Lev Bronstein to a Jewish farming family in Ukraine. He took the name Trotsky from a jailer. He was a leader of the Petrograd Soviet in 1917 and helped to organize the October uprising. As People’s Commissar for War he built the Red Army and won the civil war.

Brilliant and sharp-tongued, he made enemies. Stalin had him expelled from the party in 1927 and sent out of the country in 1929. Trotsky wrote books against Stalin from exile. In August 1940 a Soviet agent struck him with an ice axe in Mexico City, and he died the next day.

THE TESTAMENT

Lenin watched all this from his sickbed with growing worry. In late December 1922 he dictated notes for the next party congress that are now called his Testament. He praised several men and found fault with each. He wrote that Trotsky was very able but too sure of himself. He wrote that Stalin, as General Secretary, had gathered “unlimited authority” into his hands, and that he was not sure Stalin would always use it with enough caution.

Then came a quarrel. Lenin’s wife, Nadezhda Krupskaya, had taken down his notes against the doctors’ orders, and Stalin shouted at her on the telephone. When Lenin heard of it, he was furious. On January 4, 1923, he added a postscript. Stalin was too rude, he wrote, and the comrades should find a way to remove him from his post and put in a man who was more patient and polite. You will read these words in this week’s first primary source.

When Lenin died, the Testament was brought before the leaders before the party congress of May 1924. It was read to a small group. Zinoviev and Kamenev feared Trotsky more than Stalin, and they argued that Stalin should keep his post. Stalin offered to resign, and they refused. The Testament was kept from the public. Ordinary Soviet citizens could not read it openly until the 1950s.

HOW STALIN WON

Stalin won in steps, and each step used the machinery of the party and not the voice of the public. First he joined with Zinoviev and Kamenev to isolate Trotsky. Trotsky was ill and away on the Black Sea coast when Lenin died, and he was given the wrong date for the funeral, so he missed it. Stalin stood by the coffin and spoke like a faithful pupil. The party then turned Lenin into an object of worship. His body was embalmed and put on display in a tomb in Red Square.

Stalin also gave the party a slogan. Trotsky said that Russia could not reach socialism unless revolutions spread to richer countries. Stalin answered that Russia could build “socialism in one country” by its own effort. The party was tired of waiting for foreign revolutions, and the slogan gave it a national task. Soon Stalin turned on his old allies. With Bukharin’s help he defeated Zinoviev and Kamenev. In 1927 the party expelled Trotsky and Zinoviev. Then Stalin turned on Bukharin, who wanted to keep the NEP, and by 1929 had removed him from the Politburo. In December 1929 the whole country marked Stalin’s fiftieth birthday with praise. He now stood alone.

COULD IT HAVE GONE ANOTHER WAY?

Notice how many small things went Stalin’s way. Lenin’s strokes struck just as he was preparing to attack Stalin before the party. Trotsky was ill and absent at the worst moment. Zinoviev and Kamenev chose to keep Stalin, because they feared a rival more than they feared him. Any one of these might have gone differently, and the history of the party would have changed.

Yet a deeper cause lay beneath the luck. The Bolsheviks had destroyed every rival party and every free newspaper, and they had banned factions inside their own ranks. That left no lawful way to choose a leader or to remove one. A republic has offices with known powers and a known way of passing them on. The Soviet state had instead a party in which the man who controlled the jobs could win. The Greek historian Polybius taught that a state lasts because of its laws and customs, and not because of the goodness of one man. In 1924 the Soviet Union had neither a lawful succession nor a check on its rulers. That left the door open to the most patient and ruthless man in the room.
