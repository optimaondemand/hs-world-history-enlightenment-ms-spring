TODAY'S FOCUS

On November 11, 1918, the guns fell silent. Within two months the leaders of the victorious nations gathered in Paris to decide what kind of peace to make. The treaty they wrote is among the most argued-over documents in modern history. This lesson asks who made it, what choices they faced, and what they could not control. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: was the Treaty of Versailles too harsh, or too weakly enforced?

THE GUNS FALL SILENT

By the autumn of 1918 Germany’s army was being pushed back, and its allies were collapsing. In late September the German commander, Erich Ludendorff, told the government to ask for peace. Germany asked President Wilson for an armisticeAR-muh-stisAn agreement to stop fighting. It is not yet a peace treaty. See the glossary box. based on his Fourteen Points. Revolution broke out at home. The Kaiser gave up his throne on November 9 and fled to the Netherlands. Two days later, at five in the morning, German delegates signed an armistice in a railway car in a forest at Compiègne. The guns stopped at eleven. The Allies had accepted Wilson’s Fourteen Points as the basis for peace, with two reservations. One concerned freedom of the seas, which Britain would not accept. The other concerned payment for damage to civilians.

About nine million soldiers had died. Whole regions of northern France and Belgium lay in ruins. But one fact would matter later. German soldiers still stood on foreign soil, and no Allied army had marched into Germany. Many Germans came to believe that their army had not truly been beaten. That belief would be used by enemies of the new German republic.

Wilson wanted a new kind of peace, based on his Fourteen Points and a League of Nations that would keep order in the world. Orlando wanted the lands that Italy had been promised for entering the war.

These aims often clashed, and the men were tired. Wilson himself fell ill with influenza in April. The bargaining was long and bitter. Wilson had called for “open covenants of peace, openly arrived at.” But the key decisions were made in private rooms, among a handful of men, and the hundreds of experts who prepared the maps and the figures saw only parts of the work.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Georges Clemenceau
ZHORZH kleh-mahn-SOH

Georges Clemenceau (1841 to 1929) was a doctor, a journalist, and a long-serving French politician. He was nicknamed “the Tiger” for his fierce temper. As a young man he had lived through the German siege of Paris in 1870 to 1871.

He became prime minister in November 1917, when France was close to exhaustion, and he refused to consider peace until victory was won. At the Paris conference he was seventy-seven. In February 1919 an assassin shot him, and he returned to work within days. His main goal was to make Germany too weak to attack France again. He pressed for French control of the Rhineland and for heavy payments from Germany. In the end he accepted a promise of British and American help instead. That promise was never kept.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
David Lloyd George
DAY-vid LLOYD jorj

David Lloyd George (1863 to 1945) was a Welsh lawyer who rose from a modest family to become prime minister of Britain in December 1916. He was a gifted speaker and a clever bargainer, and some called him “the Welsh Wizard.”

At the conference he stood between Clemenceau, who wanted a harsh peace, and Wilson, who wanted an idealistic one. He wanted Germany to pay and lose its fleet and its colonies, but he feared a Germany pushed too far. In March 1919 he warned his colleagues that a Germany that felt unjustly treated would look for revenge. He later helped to soften some terms, but the treaty remained heavy.

He remained a leading figure in Britain until 1922.

WHAT THE TREATY SAID

The Treaty of Versailles was signed on June 28, 1919, in the Hall of Mirrors at the palace of Versailles. Its terms were heavy. Germany returned Alsace and Lorraine to France. It gave up land to Belgium and Denmark and gave much of its eastern territory to a restored Poland. It lost all its overseas colonies. Its army was limited to 100,000 men, with no tanks, no military aircraft, and no submarines. Its western territory along the Rhine was to be demilitarizeddee-MIL-ih-tuh-ryzTo remove soldiers and fortresses from an area, and to forbid their return. See the glossary box.. Allied troops would occupy parts of the Rhineland for fifteen years. The coal-rich Saar region was placed under League control, with its coal going to France. Union with Austria was forbidden. In all, Germany lost about a tenth of its people and an eighth of its land in Europe.

Article 231, which Germans called the “war guilt clause,” said that Germany and its allies were responsible for all the loss and damage of the war. On that basis Germany had to pay reparationsrep-uh-RAY-shunzPayments that a defeated nation must make to cover the damage it caused in a war. See the glossary box.. In 1921 the total was set at 132 billion gold marks, a huge sum.

The treaty also tried to apply Wilson’s principle of self-determinationself dih-TER-mih-NAY-shunThe idea that a people has the right to choose its own government and its own nation. See the glossary box.. New nations appeared, such as Poland and Czechoslovakia. But it was impossible to draw borders that matched peoples perfectly, and millions of Germans, Hungarians, and others found themselves in foreign states. Wilson’s principle did not extend to all peoples. A Japanese proposal to affirm racial equality won a majority of votes at the conference, but Wilson ruled that it needed to be unanimous, and it failed. China was angered when Germany’s rights in Shandong went to Japan, and protests began in Beijing on May 4, 1919.

🏯 CITY
Versailles

Versailles lies about twelve miles southwest of Paris. Louis XIV, the Sun King, turned it from a hunting lodge into the grandest palace in Europe in the later 1600s. Its Hall of Mirrors is a long gallery lined with huge windows and mirrors.

In January 1871, as the Germans besieged Paris, Prussia’s king was proclaimed German Emperor in this hall. For France it was a deep humiliation. In 1919 the French insisted that the peace treaty be signed in the same hall, on June 28, five years to the day after the assassination at Sarajevo. The setting was a deliberate reply to 1871. Many Germans saw it as an insult.

SIGNED UNDER A THREAT

Germany had not been allowed to negotiate. When German leaders saw the terms in May, they were outraged. Many Germans called the treaty a DiktatDIK-tahtA German word for a settlement dictated to the loser without real negotiation. Germans used it as a name for the Treaty of Versailles. See the glossary box., a dictated peace. The chancellor, Philipp Scheidemann, asked whether any hand would not wither that signed it, and his government resigned rather than sign. The Allies warned that they would resume the war. On June 21 German sailors at Scapa Flow sank their own fleet, which had been held in British hands. A new government gave way, and two German ministers signed.

Marshal Foch, the Allied commander, is said to have remarked, “This is not peace. It is an armistice for twenty years.” Whether or not he said it, many people shared the feeling. The treaty left Germany angry, but it left Germany still large. Its people numbered more than sixty million, against France’s forty million, and its factories and coalfields were largely intact.

Here lies the heart of the argument you will take up this week. Some say the treaty was too harsh to be forgiven. Others say it was too weak to be enforced. And some say it was less harsh than the peace Germany itself had forced on Russia a year before. A treaty is only as strong as the will of the nations that enforce it.
