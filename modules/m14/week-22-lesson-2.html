TODAY'S FOCUS

The Treaty of Versailles was only one of several treaties. The war had destroyed four great empires and left their lands up for grabs. In this lesson you will see what replaced them. You will also see how the United States, whose president had done so much to shape the peace, refused to join the League of Nations he had designed.

FOUR EMPIRES GONE

Before 1914 four empires ruled much of Europe and the Middle East. By 1919 all had fallen. The German Empire became a republic. The Russian Empire had become Bolshevik Russia. The Austro-Hungarian Empire broke apart in the autumn of 1918, and Emperor Charles gave up his role in government. The Ottoman Empire, the great Muslim power that had lasted six hundred years, lay in defeat.

New nations took their places. Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria, Hungary, and the Kingdom of the Serbs, Croats, and Slovenes, later called Yugoslavia, were formed from the old Habsburg lands and neighbors. Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania broke away from Russia. Hungary lost about two-thirds of its land in a separate treaty in 1920. Austria became a small republic of about six million people. Many Austrians wished to join Germany, but the treaties forbade it.

In the Middle East, Britain and France divided much of the old Ottoman lands. Under the League’s system of mandatesMAN-daytLand taken from a defeated empire and given to a victorious nation to govern for a time, under the League of Nations. During the war Britain had made promises to Arab leaders and also promised a national home for Jews in Palestine. These promises conflicted, and the conflict would last. A Turkish army led by Mustafa Kemal, whom you met at Gallipoli, rejected the first plan for dividing Turkey itself. A new treaty in 1923 recognized the modern Turkish republic.

Many of the new nations were small and weak. They sat between a resentful Germany and a Russia that was now Communist. Each held minorities who felt like strangers. About three million Germans lived in the new Czechoslovakia, for instance. The peacemakers had made a new map without making new strength. That would matter in 1938.

WILSON'S LEAGUE

For Wilson the most important part of the peace was the League of Nations. Its Covenant, or constitution, was built into the Treaty of Versailles. Member nations promised to settle disputes peacefully. Article 10 went further. It pledged members to respect and defend one another’s borders against attack. This idea is called collective securitykuh-LEK-tiv sih-KYUR-ih-teeAn arrangement in which many nations promise to defend one another against an attacker. See the glossary box.. The League would meet in Geneva and have an assembly of all members and a council of the great powers.

The League held its first Assembly in Geneva in 1920. Germany joined in 1926, and the Soviet Union in 1934. But the League had no army. To act against an aggressor, it had to persuade its members to impose penalties, and each member would have to agree. Its strength depended entirely on the will of its great powers.

THE SENATE SAYS NO

Under the American Constitution, a treaty needs the consent of two-thirds of the Senate. In America the process of approving a treaty is called ratificationRAT-ih-fyTo approve a treaty formally, so that it takes effect. See the glossary box.. Republicans controlled the Senate in 1919, and the chairman of its Foreign Relations Committee was Henry Cabot Lodge. LodgeHEN-ree KAB-ut LOJHenry Cabot Lodge was the Republican senator who led the Senate fight against Wilson’s version of the Treaty of Versailles. Read more below. disliked Wilson and disliked Article 10. He feared that it could commit American soldiers to fight without a vote of Congress. He proposed a list of changes, called reservations. A small group of senators, called the irreconcilables, opposed any League at all. A larger group wanted only small changes. Wilson’s refusal to bargain kept that middle group from joining his side.

Wilson refused to compromise. In September 1919 he took his case to the people on a speaking tour across the country, traveling about eight thousand miles. On September 25 he collapsed in Colorado, and a week later he suffered a severe stroke. From his sickbed he urged Democratic senators to vote against the treaty if it came with Lodge’s reservations. On November 19, 1919, the Senate voted down the treaty with the reservations, and then voted down the treaty without them. In March 1920 a last attempt fell short again.

It is one of history’s great what-ifs. The two sides were not far apart. If Wilson had accepted a compromise, the treaty might have passed. The United States made a separate peace with Germany in 1921. A promised American and British guarantee of France’s safety never took effect. France was left to rely on its own strength.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Henry Cabot Lodge
HEN-ree KAB-ut LOJ

Henry Cabot Lodge (1850 to 1924) was a senator from Massachusetts for more than thirty years. He was a scholar with a doctorate from Harvard, the author of many books, and a close friend of Theodore Roosevelt. In 1919 he led the Republican majority in the Senate and chaired the Foreign Relations Committee.

Lodge did not oppose all international cooperation. He supported joining the war, and he wanted Germany punished. But he distrusted Article 10 of the League Covenant because it might commit the United States to wars it did not choose. He proposed fourteen reservations, a number some people said was meant to mock Wilson’s Fourteen Points.

Wilson and Lodge disliked each other deeply, and historians still debate how much that personal quarrel shaped the outcome.

THE ECONOMIST'S WARNING

The treaty also met opposition from a young British economist, John Maynard Keynes. He had served at the conference as an adviser to the British Treasury. In June 1919, in despair, he resigned. That winter he published a book, The Economic Consequences of the Peace. It argued that the reparations were impossible to pay and that crushing Germany’s economy would drag down all of Europe. The book became a bestseller. You will read a part of it in this week’s second primary source.

Later historians have argued that Keynes exaggerated. They point out that Germany paid only a fraction of what was demanded and borrowed heavily from American banks. Some say Germany could have paid more if it had wished. Others say the real problem was that no one enforced the treaty. But Keynes’s book shaped public opinion in Britain and America. Many people came to think the treaty was unjust, and that made it harder to enforce.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
John Maynard Keynes
JON MAY-nard KAYNZ

John Maynard Keynes (1883 to 1946) was an economist from Cambridge, England. During the war he worked at the British Treasury, and in 1919 he went to Paris as an adviser. He was brilliant and sharp-tongued, and he grew angry as he watched the leaders bargain.

He resigned in June 1919 and wrote his book at great speed. It described the peacemakers in memorable and often unkind portraits. Keynes warned that the treaty would impoverish Europe and breed a desire for revenge.

Later he became the most famous economist of his century. In the 1930s he argued that governments should spend money to fight unemployment. His ideas influenced many nations, including Roosevelt’s America, which you will study next week.

TOO HARSH OR TOO WEAK?

Now you are ready for this week’s question. One view says the peace was too harsh. It left Germany angry and gave extremists a cause. Another view says it was too weakly enforced. The winners lost their nerve, quarrelled with one another, and let Germany slip out of its limits. A third view reminds us that the peacemakers faced hard choices. A harsher peace might have needed a long occupation that none of their voters would accept.

Whichever view you favor, notice how many separate decisions fed the outcome. The armistice, the choice of terms, Wilson’s stubbornness, and the Senate’s vote might each have gone another way. Nothing was fated. As you read this week’s sources, ask who made each choice and what else they might have done.
