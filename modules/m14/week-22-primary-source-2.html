PRIMARY SOURCE 2
Keynes: The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919)
An economist who attended the conference explains why he thinks the treaty will fail.
💡 BEFORE YOU READ

John Maynard Keynes was a British economist who worked for the Treasury and went to Paris in 1919 as an adviser. He resigned in June, before the treaty was signed, and published this book that December. It sold widely in Britain and America.

Keynes is not a neutral witness. He disliked the treaty and he wrote with feeling. His picture of the conference is also one man’s view of what happened inside the room. Keep that in mind as you read, and ask what evidence he gives for his claims and what he expects will happen.

A few terms may be new. A “Carthaginian Peace” means a peace that tries to destroy the defeated nation, as Rome destroyed the city of Carthage after the Punic Wars. “M. Clemenceau” is Georges Clemenceau, the French prime minister. “The President” is Woodrow Wilson. “Central Europe” means Germany and its neighbors. “Guarantees” were the safeguards, such as occupation of the Rhineland, that France insisted on to protect itself.

Read it as a historian should. Ask who wrote it, when, and for whom, and ask what Keynes thinks the peacemakers should have cared about.

This is the policy of an old man, whose most vivid impressions and most lively imagination are of the past and not of the future.

[...]

It happens, however, that it is not only an ideal question that is at issue. My purpose in this book is to show that the Carthaginian Peace is not practically right or possible. Although the school of thought from which it springs is aware of the economic factor, it overlooks, nevertheless, the deeper economic tendencies which are to govern the future. The clock cannot be set back. You cannot restore Central Europe to 1870 without setting up such strains in the European structure and letting loose such human and spiritual forces as, pushing beyond frontiers and races, will overwhelm not only you and your “guarantees,” but your institutions, and the existing order of your Society.

[...]

The thoughts which I have expressed in the second chapter were not present to the mind of Paris. The future life of Europe was not their concern; its means of livelihood was not their anxiety. Their preoccupations, good and bad alike, related to frontiers and nationalities, to the balance of power, to imperial aggrandizements, to the future enfeeblement of a strong and dangerous enemy, to revenge, and to the shifting by the victors of their unbearable financial burdens on to the shoulders of the defeated.

Two rival schemes for the future polity of the world took the field,--the Fourteen Points of the President, and the Carthaginian Peace of M. Clemenceau. Yet only one of these was entitled to take the field; for the enemy had not surrendered unconditionally, but on agreed terms as to the general character of the Peace.

📌 A NOTE FOR YOU

Keynes writes as a critic and an advocate. Notice the words he chooses to describe each side, and notice the claim he makes in the sentence that begins “The clock cannot be set back.” You can return to this page as often as you like.
