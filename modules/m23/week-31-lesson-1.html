TODAY'S FOCUS

On August 6, 1945, an American bomber dropped a single bomb on a Japanese city. Three days later it dropped a second. This lesson tells how that came about, and how the war ended. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: was Truman right to use the atomic bomb? This is a question on which thoughtful people still disagree, and the lesson gives you the evidence on every side.

THE SHRINKING RING

By the spring of 1945 Japan had lost most of its navy and many of its islands. From bases in the Marianas, American bombers struck Japanese cities. On the night of March 9 and 10, a fire raid on Tokyo burned much of the city and killed about 100,000 people. Japan’s leaders did not give in. The fighting for Iwo Jima in February and March cost nearly 7,000 American lives. On Okinawa, which began on April 1, about 12,500 Americans died. More than 100,000 Japanese soldiers died there as well, and so did a very large number of Okinawan civilians.

The next step was to invade the home islands. Plans called for a landing on the southern island of Kyushu in November 1945, and another near Tokyo in the spring. The Japanese army moved its best units to the beaches and trained boys and women to fight with sharpened bamboo and old rifles. Estimates of how many Americans would die in an invasion varied widely, and they are still argued over. No one doubted that the cost would be very high, for Americans, and also for the Japanese who would defend their homeland.

THE BOMB

In August 1939 two refugee physicists, Leo Szilard and Albert Einstein, warned Roosevelt in a letter that Germany might build a bomb from this discovery. The United States began a secret program in 1942 called the Manhattan Project. General Leslie Groves ran it, and the physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer led the laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico. It cost about two billion dollars, and it employed more than 100,000 people, most of whom did not know what they were building.

Germany had no bomb, and it had surrendered in May. But the work went on. On July 16, 1945, scientists exploded the first atomic bomb in the desert near Alamogordo, New Mexico. The blast was far greater than many expected. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Harry Truman
HAIR-ee TROO-mun

Harry Truman (1884 to 1972) grew up on a Missouri farm and served as an artillery officer in the Great War. He failed in business and then entered politics, and he reached the Senate in 1935. He earned respect there by looking into waste in military spending.

He was Roosevelt’s vice president for only eighty-two days. When Roosevelt died, he took office knowing little about the secret bomb project or the plans for the peace. He was plain-spoken and decisive, and a sign on his desk read that the buck stops here. He later led the country into the Cold War and the Korean War, and he chose not to run again in 1952.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The Atomic Bomb

An atomic bomb works by a chain reaction. When the center of certain heavy atoms is split, it sends out small particles that split other atoms, and so on, at great speed. In a fraction of a second, a small amount of matter turns into a great release of heat and force.

The project produced two kinds of bomb. One used uranium and was a simple gun-type design. It was never tested before it was used on Hiroshima. The other used plutonium, a new element made in nuclear reactors, and it was a complex design that had to be tested first at Alamogordo. The blast also gave off radiation, which sickened and killed people in the days and years afterward. This was a danger that the scientists knew about but had not fully measured.

THE DECISION

In June a committee of advisers told Truman that the bomb should be used against Japan as soon as it was ready, without warning, on a military target surrounded by workers’ homes. Others disagreed. Some scientists asked for a demonstration on an empty island. Some officials urged a change in the terms of surrender, to let Japan keep its emperor. Some believed that Japan might give in once the Soviet Union joined the war, since Stalin had promised at Yalta to attack three months after Germany fell.

On July 26 the United States, Britain, and China issued the Potsdam Declaration. Truman had already approved the order to use the bomb after August 3. He believed that the war could be ended faster this way than by an invasion.

American codebreakers could read some of Japan’s diplomatic messages, and they showed that Tokyo was asking the Soviets to arrange a settlement. But they also showed that Japan’s leaders still would not accept unconditional surrender, and that the army meant to fight on at home. Truman had to weigh what he could see of Japan’s intentions against what he feared about the cost of an invasion.

HIROSHIMA AND NAGASAKI

At 8:15 on the morning of August 6, an American B-29 named the Enola Gay dropped a uranium bomb on Hiroshima, a city of about 350,000 that held an army headquarters. About 70,000 to 80,000 people died at once. THE SURRENDER

Even now Japan’s Supreme Council split three against three. Some leaders would accept the Potsdam terms if the emperor kept his place, and others wanted to fight on. On the night of August 9 and 10, Emperor Hirohitoheer-oh-HEE-tohHirohito was the emperor of Japan from 1926 to 1989, whose intervention in August 1945 brought the war to an end. Read more below. broke the tie, and he told the council to “bear the unbearable.” The Allies replied that the emperor would be under the authority of the Supreme Commander in Japan. After another debate, the emperor ruled again for surrender. On August 15 the Japanese people heard his voice on the radio for the first time. A group of officers had tried to seize the recording the night before, but they failed. The formal surrender was signed on September 2 on the battleship Missouri in Tokyo Bay.

THE ARGUMENT

Historians have given two main answers. Some say that the bombs ended the war quickly, saved the lives of many Americans and Japanese who would have died in an invasion, and saved the many people dying each week across Asia under Japanese rule. Others say that Japan was close to collapse, that the Soviet entry or a promise about the emperor might have been enough, and that killing so many civilians on purpose was wrong. Each side must guess what would have happened if the bomb had not been used, and no one can know. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Emperor Hirohito
heer-oh-HEE-toh

Hirohito (1901 to 1989) became emperor in 1926. In the Japanese tradition he was a sacred figure who reigned above politics, and the cabinet and the generals ran the government in his name. How much he shaped the decisions of the 1930s is still argued over.

In August 1945 he stepped outside that custom and broke the deadlock in the council, because the government could not decide by itself. After the surrender the American general Douglas MacArthur chose to keep him on the throne, which helped the occupation go smoothly. In 1946 Hirohito gave up the claim that he was a god. He reigned until his death in 1989.
