TODAY'S FOCUS

When the guns stopped, the winners faced two questions. What should be done with the men who had ruled Germany and Japan? And what could keep a third great war from coming? This lesson looks at the trial at Nuremberg and at the founding of the United Nations. Both rested on the hope that law could do what force alone had failed to do. Keep that hope in mind when you read this week’s second primary source.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Robert Jackson
ROB-ert JAK-sun

Robert Jackson (1892 to 1954) was a self-taught lawyer from upstate New York who never attended a full law school course. He served as Attorney General under Roosevelt, and in 1941 Roosevelt put him on the Supreme Court.

He took leave from the Court to lead the American prosecution at Nuremberg, and he shaped the case and the charter. He was a gifted speaker, and his opening speech is remembered as one of the best of the century. The work was hard, and it caused quarrels with the other prosecutors and with his own colleagues. He returned to the Court in 1946 and served until his death.

🏯 CITY
Nuremberg

Nuremberg is a city in Bavaria, in southern Germany, that was a great center of trade and craft in the Middle Ages. The Nazis chose it for their yearly party rallies, which drew hundreds of thousands, and in 1935 they announced the race laws there.

In January 1945 Allied bombers destroyed most of the old city. The Palace of Justice, a large courthouse on the edge of town, was damaged but could be repaired in a few months. The trial began in its largest courtroom in November 1945. The choice of the city was meant to show that the regime had ended where it had made its show of strength.

The trial opened on November 20, 1945. Twenty-four men were named in the indictmentin-DYT-mentA formal written charge that says what crimes a person is accused of. Jackson’s opening speech on November 21 told the court why the trial mattered, and you will read part of it this week. Most of the proof came from the Nazis themselves, in thousands of captured documents. On November 29 the court watched films of the camps, shot by Allied soldiers. The defendants were allowed lawyers, and they could call witnesses and question those who testified against them. Most of them said that they had only followed orders. The tribunal rejected that defense, though it allowed it to be weighed when deciding the sentence.

The defense made two serious objections. The first was that the charge of crimes against peace was an ex post factoEKS pohst FAK-tohA Latin phrase meaning after the fact. The old rule nullum crimen sine legeNUL-um KRIM-en SIN-ay LAY-gayA Latin phrase meaning no crime without law. See the glossary box. says that no one may be punished for something that was not a crime when it was done. The tribunal answered that Germany had signed the Kellogg-Briand Pact of 1928, which gave up war as a way of settling quarrels, and that justice would be poorly served if the guilty went free on a technicality. Some American leaders, such as Senator Robert Taft, still thought the principle had been broken.

The second objection was that it was victors’ justice. The Soviet Union had joined Hitler in attacking Poland, and it had shot Polish officers at Katyn. The Soviet prosecutors tried to blame Katyn on the Germans, and the court did not accept it. The Allies had also bombed cities. A fair observer must grant that the tribunal was not perfect. But it heard evidence, and it gave every man a defense, which was more than the Nazis had given their victims.

THE VERDICTS

The verdicts came on October 1, 1946. Twelve men were sentenced to death, three to life in prison, and four to shorter terms, and three were found not guilty. Göring took poison in his cell a few hours before he was to be hanged. The rest of the condemned were executed on October 16. Later American courts at Nuremberg tried doctors, judges, and business leaders. In Tokyo a similar tribunal tried Japan’s leaders from 1946 to 1948, and seven men, among them Hideki Tojo, were hanged. In its judgment the Nuremberg tribunal said that crimes are committed by men and not by abstract entities, and that a person has duties that go beyond obedience to his own state. These trials set a principle that leaders can be held to account for crimes against peace and humanity, though many of the same questions about fairness came up there too.

THE UNITED NATIONS

The second hope was an organization to keep the peace. You have read how the League of Nations failed, partly because the American Senate refused to join. Roosevelt had planned a new body during the war, and in April 1945 delegates from fifty nations met in San Francisco to write its charterCHAR-terA written document that creates an organization and sets out its purposes and rules. See the glossary box.. It was signed on June 26. The Senate approved it on July 28 by a vote of 89 to 2, and it took effect on October 24, 1945.

All members sit in a General Assembly, but the real power lies in the Security Council. It has five permanent members, the United States, Britain, France, the Soviet Union, and China, and each holds a vetoVEE-tohThe power to block a decision by saying no. See the glossary box.. Any one of them can block action. This was a price paid for the great powers’ membership, because they would not join otherwise. But it meant that the United Nations could not act against any of them. The founders were realistic about this, since they knew that peace could not be kept by a body that the strongest states would not obey.

The Assembly also created agencies for food, health, and refugees, and in 1948 it adopted a Universal Declaration of Human Rights, though the declaration had no power to enforce itself. Look back at the two efforts of this lesson. Each tried to bind the strong to a rule of law, and each depended on the strong agreeing to be bound. As you read Jackson’s words this week, ask whether law that rests on the consent of the powerful is a weakness or a beginning.
