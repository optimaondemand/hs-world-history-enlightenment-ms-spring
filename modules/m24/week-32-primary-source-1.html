PRIMARY SOURCE 1
Kennan, the Long Telegram (February 22, 1946)
An American diplomat in Moscow explains how the Soviet leaders think.
💡 BEFORE YOU READ

George Kennan sent this message from the American embassy in Moscow to the State Department in Washington. It was a reply to a question about Soviet intentions. At about eight thousand words it was far longer than most telegrams, and it was read by the Secretary of State, the president, and many other officials. Copies were passed around the government, and it made Kennan’s name.

Telegrams were paid for by the word, so writers dropped small words such as “the” and “a.” You will see that in the text. A few terms may be new. “Kremlin” means the Soviet government, which met in a fortress in Moscow. “Neurotic” means driven by fear. “Modus vivendi” is a Latin phrase for a working arrangement. “Schematic” means following a fixed plan. “Adventuristic” means taking wild risks.

Read this as a historian should. Ask what Kennan says is the root of Soviet behavior. Ask how he says the Soviets will react to strength and to weakness. Ask what he thinks the American task is.

At bottom of Kremlin’s neurotic view of world affairs is traditional and instinctive Russian sense of insecurity.

[...]

We have here a political force committed fanatically to the belief that with US there can be no permanent modus vivendi, that it is desirable and necessary that the internal harmony of our society be disrupted, our traditional way of life be destroyed, the international authority of our state be broken, if Soviet power is to be secure.

[...]

Soviet power, unlike that of Hitlerite Germany, is neither schematic nor adventuristic. It does not work by fixed plans. It does not take unnecessary risks. Impervious to logic of reason, and it is highly sensitive to logic of force. For this reason it can easily withdraw -- and usually does when strong resistance is encountered at any point.

[...]

Problem of how to cope with this force is undoubtedly greatest task our diplomacy has ever faced and probably greatest it will ever have to face.

📌 A NOTE FOR YOU

Notice that Kennan gives two reasons for Soviet behavior, one drawn from Russian history and one from Communist belief. Ask which one he treats as deeper, and what he says about force. Notice also how he ends. You can return to this page as often as you like.
