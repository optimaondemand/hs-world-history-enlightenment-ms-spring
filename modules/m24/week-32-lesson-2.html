THE MARSHALL PLAN

Europe in 1947 was hungry and worn out. Cities lay in ruins, and the winter of 1946 and 1947 was one of the coldest on record. Factories could not run without coal, and farms could not feed the towns. Communist parties were strong in France and Italy, and many Americans feared that despair would carry them to power. The Truman Doctrine had promised help to a few countries. It could not heal a whole continent.

On June 5, 1947, Secretary of State George MarshallJORJ MAR-shulGeorge Marshall was the American general who led the army in the Second World War and, as Secretary of State, gave his name to the plan for European recovery. Read more below. gave a short speech at Harvard. He said that American policy was “directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos.” He invited the nations of Europe to draw up a joint plan for their recovery and promised American help. The offer included the Soviet Union and the nations of the East. In July the Soviet foreign minister, Molotov, came to Paris for talks and walked out. Stalin feared that the aid would draw his satellites toward the West, so he forced them to refuse. Czechoslovakia had accepted the invitation, and Moscow made its government take it back.

Congress passed the aid bill in April 1948. Over four years the United States gave about 13 billion dollars to sixteen countries. The money bought food, fuel, and machinery, and it came with a push for the nations to work together. Europe’s factories started up again. The Marshall Plan was the economic side of containmentkun-TAYN-mentThe American policy of stopping the spread of Soviet power by firm pressure in every place it advanced. See the glossary box.. The Soviets answered in September 1947 by forming the KominformKOM-in-formA short name for the Communist Information Bureau, a Soviet agency begun in 1947 to unite the Communist parties of Europe under Moscow’s lead. See the glossary box., an agency that tied the Communist parties of Europe to Moscow’s leadership.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
George Marshall
JORJ MAR-shul

George Marshall (1880 to 1959) graduated from the Virginia Military Institute. As army chief of staff from 1939 to 1945 he built the army from fewer than 200,000 men into a force of eight million. Churchill called him the organizer of victory, and Roosevelt trusted his judgment so much that he named him to lead the army instead of sending him to command the invasion of France.

He was known for his calm, his plain speech, and his refusal to seek credit. After the war Truman sent him to China to seek peace between the Nationalists and Communists, and that mission failed. In 1947 he became Secretary of State, and in 1953 he won the Nobel Peace Prize for the plan that bears his name.

TWO GERMANYS

The quarrel centered on Germany. Each power had been taking reparationsrep-uh-RAY-shunzPayments, or goods and labor, that a defeated nation must give to the winners to make up for war damage. See the glossary box. from its own zone, and the West did not want its zones starved. The British and American zones were merged in 1947, and the French joined them in 1948. In February 1948 the Communists seized power in Prague, and Czechoslovakia, the last free government in the East, became a satellite stateSAT-uh-lyt STAYTA country that is officially independent but is controlled by a stronger neighbor. See the glossary box.. The Western powers took this as proof of Soviet aims. On June 20 they introduced a new currency, the Deutsche Mark, in their zones to stop inflation and restart trade. The Soviets saw this as the first step to a separate West German state.

THE BERLIN BLOCKADE

On June 24, 1948, the Soviets cut all road, rail, and canal links between West Germany and West Berlin. This was a blockadeblok-AYDThe shutting off of a place by force so that people and goods cannot go in or out. See the glossary box., and it left about 2.2 million people in the Western half of the city with food and coal for only a few weeks. Stalin hoped to push the Western powers out of Berlin, or at least to stop the plan for a West German state. Some of Truman’s advisers urged an armed convoy, which risked war. Truman refused to abandon the city.

The answer was an airliftAIR-liftThe moving of people or supplies by aircraft when other routes are closed. See the glossary box., the LuftbrückeLOOFT-brook-uhA German word for air bridge. Berliners used it as the name for the airlift that supplied their city in 1948 and 1949. See the glossary box.. Planes landed in Berlin every few minutes, day and night, bringing in food, coal, and even the machinery for a power plant. On its best day, in April 1949, over 1,300 flights landed. In all about 2.3 million tons were carried in fifteen months. An American pilot, Gail Halvorsen, began dropping candy to children by small parachutes. In the end the Soviet Union lifted the blockade on May 12, 1949. Notice what the blockade did, and what it cost. It showed the Soviets as bullies to a watching world, and it made the airlift a proof that the West would stay. It was meant to stop a West German state, and it drove West Germans and the Western powers closer together.

🏯 CITY
Berlin

Berlin was the capital of Germany, a great city of about four million before the war. By 1945 much of it was rubble from bombing and from the fight in April. The Soviet army took it, and under the Yalta agreement the city was divided into American, British, French, and Soviet sectors.

The city lay about a hundred miles inside the Soviet zone, which made the three Western sectors an island. Only a few roads, rails, canals, and three air corridors led to it. This made Berlin easy to squeeze, and also a symbol of Western will. The city remained divided until 1990.

THE EASTERN ANSWER

Moscow called the Marshall Plan an attempt to buy Europe with dollars. Its own answer was to tie the East together. In January 1949 the Soviet Union and its satellites formed the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, to trade with one another on terms that Moscow set. Stalin could not control every Communist, however. In June 1948 Yugoslavia’s leader, Josip Tito, refused to follow Moscow’s orders, and the Kominform threw his country out. Tito kept power, and he kept Yugoslavia independent of both camps. The split showed that the Soviet grip was firm but not complete, and that Communists could quarrel with one another.

NATO AND THE WARSAW PACT

The blockade frightened Western Europe. On April 4, 1949, twelve nations signed the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington, among them the United States, Canada, Britain, France, and Italy. The heart of it was Article 5, which said that an armed attack on one would be treated as an attack on all. This was collective defensekuh-LEK-tiv dih-FENSAn agreement among nations that an attack on one of them will be treated as an attack on all. See the glossary box.. For the first time since 1778 the United States had joined a peacetime alliance with European powers, and it broke the old advice to avoid such entanglements.

The division of Germany followed. The Federal Republic of Germany, or West Germany, was founded in May 1949, and the Soviets answered in October with the German Democratic Republic, or East Germany. In August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its own atomic bomb. In May 1955 West Germany joined NATO, and five days later the Soviet Union and seven satellite states signed the Warsaw Pact. Europe now had two armed blocsBLOKA group of nations that act together for shared goals. See the glossary box.. The two superpowersSOO-per-pow-erA nation so strong in arms and wealth that it can shape events around the world. See the glossary box. each led one. The line between them ran through the middle of Germany, as the Iron Curtain.

WHO DIVIDED EUROPE?

Historians do not agree. Some put the blame on Stalin, whose control of Eastern Europe, the coup in Prague, and the blockade pushed the West to unite. Others stress that American money, the new currency, and a West German state looked to Moscow like a threat. Still others say that the armies’ positions in 1945 had already drawn the line.
