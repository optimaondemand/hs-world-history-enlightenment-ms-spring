TODAY'S FOCUS

The Soviet Union, Britain, and the United States had fought Germany together. Within two years they were rivals who spoke of each other as enemies. This lesson follows the conferences at Yalta and Potsdam, the spread of Soviet power over Eastern Europe, and the American reply. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: who divided Europe?

YALTA

In February 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin met at Yalta, a resort on the Black Sea in the Crimea. The Red Army was already about fifty miles from Berlin, and the war in Europe was nearly won. Roosevelt was ill, and he would die in two months. The leaders agreed that Germany would be divided into four zones, American, British, Soviet, and French, and that Berlin, deep in the Soviet zone, would be divided in the same way. They agreed that Germany would pay reparationsrep-uh-RAY-shunzPayments, or goods and labor, that a defeated nation must give to the winners to make up for war damage. See the glossary box., and that the Soviet Union would take much of them.

The hardest question was Poland. The Soviets had taken the eastern part of it in 1939 and meant to keep it, and Poland would be paid with German land in the west. The three leaders said that the Polish government would be widened and that free elections would be held. The Declaration on Liberated Europe made the same promise for all the lands freed from Germany. Stalin also promised to join the war against Japan within three months of the defeat of Germany. And the leaders settled the question of the vetoVEE-tohThe power to block a decision by saying no. See the glossary box. in the Security Council of the new United Nations.

Later critics said that Roosevelt gave Eastern Europe away. Defenders say that the Red Army already held it, and that the only thing the West held was a promise. Notice that both sides of the argument agree on one thing: the promise of free elections was not kept.

POTSDAM

The leaders met again at Potsdam, near Berlin, from July 17 to August 2. Roosevelt was dead, and Truman had taken his place. Churchill was voted out of office in the middle of the conference, and Clement Attlee took his seat. Germany had given up by unconditional surrenderun-kun-DISH-un-ul suh-REN-derA surrender in which the defeated side gets no promises in return and accepts whatever terms the winner sets. See the glossary box., and there was no German government left to talk to. The three powers agreed to rule it together through a council of the four zone commanders, to disarm it, and to try its war criminals.

The quarrels were already sharp. The Soviets were stripping their zone of factories and railways as reparationsrep-uh-RAY-shunzPayments, or goods and labor, that a defeated nation must give to the winners to make up for war damage. See the glossary box.. Stalin put the new Polish border at the Oder and Neisse rivers, and about twelve million Germans were driven out of the lands in the east. Truman was more suspicious of Stalin than Roosevelt had been. He hinted to Stalin that the United States had a powerful new weapon, and Stalin showed little surprise. He had already learned of it through spies.

STALIN'S EUROPE

The Red Army stayed where it was, and local Communists, backed by Moscow, took over one government after another. In Poland the elections of 1947 were rigged. In Romania and Bulgaria Communist ministers took control of the police and the army and drove out their rivals. By 1948 these countries were satellite statesSAT-uh-lyt STAYTA country that is officially independent but is controlled by a stronger neighbor. See the glossary box.. Stalin had reasons of his own. His country had been invaded twice in a generation and had lost more than twenty million people. He wanted a belt of friendly countries on his western border. He was also a convinced Communist who saw a chance to spread his system, and historians still argue over how far each motive drove him.

In March 1946, at Fulton, Missouri, Churchill told an American audience that “from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic an iron curtain has descended across the Continent.” The phrase, the iron curtainEYE-urn KUR-tunChurchill’s name for the line that divided Soviet-controlled Eastern Europe from the free nations of the West. See the glossary box., stuck. Soviet troops also stayed in northern Iran until the spring of 1946, and Stalin pressed Turkey for control of the straits that lead from the Black Sea. Each of these moves alarmed Washington. The United States and the Soviet Union had become the two superpowersSOO-per-pow-erA nation so strong in arms and wealth that it can shape events around the world. See the glossary box., and no one else was close to their strength.

KENNAN'S TELEGRAM

In February 1946 the State Department asked its man in Moscow why the Soviets acted as they did. The answer came from George KennanJORJ KEN-unGeorge Kennan was the American diplomat whose telegram from Moscow in 1946 laid out the policy of containment. Read more below., in a telegram of about eight thousand words, and you will read part of it this week. He argued that Soviet leaders needed to believe that the outside world was hostile, to justify their harsh rule at home. He argued that they were patient and that they would push where the push was easy, but that they would stop when met with firm resistance.

His advice became the policy of containmentkun-TAYN-mentThe American policy of stopping the spread of Soviet power by firm pressure in every place it advanced. See the glossary box.. The United States should not try to roll back Soviet power by force, and it should not give way. It should resist each advance, wherever it came, with patient and steady pressure until the Soviet system changed. Kennan later set out the argument in a magazine article, signed only with the letter X. The idea guided American policy for forty years.

George Kennan
JORJ KEN-un

George Kennan (1904 to 2005) was born in Wisconsin and joined the diplomatic service as a young man. He studied Russian and served in Moscow when the American embassy first opened there in 1933. He knew the language and history of Russia better than almost any official in Washington.

His telegram of 1946 made his name, and in 1947 he became the head of the State Department’s planning staff. Later he feared that his idea had been turned into a program of arms and alliances, when he had meant it to be mostly a contest of political and economic strength. He left government in the early 1950s and became a historian. He lived to be 101.

GREECE, TURKEY, AND THE TRUMAN DOCTRINE

The test came in the eastern Mediterranean. Greece was fighting a civil war against Communist guerrillas, and Turkey was under Soviet pressure. Britain had held both up with money and arms, but in February 1947 it told Washington that it could no longer afford to do so. On March 12 Truman went before Congress and asked for 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey. He said that “it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures.” You will read his speech this week.

The speech put the quarrel in the plainest terms, as a choice between two ways of life. Congress approved the money, and Greece held. Some critics said that Truman overstated the case, because Stalin was not directing the Greek rebels. But the speech set the course of American policy. For the first time in its history the United States had promised, in time of peace, to help other nations resist pressure from a great power.
