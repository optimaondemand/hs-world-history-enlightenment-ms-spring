CASTRO'S REVOLUTION

Cuba was ruled until 1959 by the dictator Fulgencio Batista, whose government was friendly to the United States and corrupt at home. A young lawyer, Fidel Castrofih-DEL KAS-trohFidel Castro was the revolutionary who ruled Cuba from 1959 and made it a Communist ally of the Soviet Union. Read more below., landed in 1956 with a handful of men and began a guerrillaguh-RIL-uhA fighter who works in small bands, using ambush and surprise against a stronger army. See the glossary box. war in the mountains. Batista’s army melted away, and on New Year’s Day of 1959 he fled the island. Castro entered Havana as a hero. He promised free elections, but he never held them.

Within two years Castro had taken over American-owned sugar mills and oil refineries and had made a deal to sell sugar to the Soviet Union. The United States ended its purchases of Cuban sugar, forbade most trade, and in January 1961 broke off relations. In December 1961 Castro announced that he was a Marxist-Leninist and that Cuba would become a Communist state. To many Americans this was a nightmare come true: a Soviet friend in the Western Hemisphere. It also seemed to fit the domino theoryDOM-ih-noh THEER-eeThe belief that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would fall one after another. See the glossary box. in Latin America.

THE BAY OF PIGS

The Eisenhower administration had already ordered the CIA to train Cuban exiles for an invasion. Kennedy took office in January 1961 and let the plan go forward, although he had doubts. On April 17 about fourteen hundred exiles landed at the Bay of PigsThe Bay of PigsThe Bay of Pigs was the swampy landing place on Cuba’s south coast where an invasion by exiles failed in April 1961. Read more below.. The planners had hoped that the Cuban people would rise to join them. They did not. Kennedy, wishing to hide the American hand, had cut back the air support, and Castro’s planes sank the supply ships. In three days the invasion was crushed and about twelve hundred men were taken prisoner. The failure was an embarrassment for a new president.

Khrushchev met Kennedy at Vienna in June 1961 and came away thinking that the young man was weak and could be pushed. Two months later East Germany built the Berlin Wall, and Kennedy did nothing to stop it.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Fidel Castro
fih-DEL KAS-troh

Fidel Castro (1926 to 2016) was born to a wealthy sugar planter and studied law in Havana. In 1953 he led an attack on an army post and was jailed. After release he went to Mexico, and in 1956 he sailed back to Cuba with eighty-two men, and most were killed. The survivors, including Castro and Che Guevara, began a guerrilla war.

Castro ruled Cuba for almost fifty years. His government gave free schooling and medical care, but it jailed its critics, closed the free press, and controlled the economy. Hundreds of thousands of Cubans fled the island. He passed power to his brother Raul in 2008.

🛡 BATTLE
The Bay of Pigs

The invaders landed at a spot called Playa Giron, on a bay on Cuba’s south coast. The planners had chosen it because it was remote and had an airstrip. The same remoteness made it hard to escape from. The beaches were backed by a swamp, and the nearest mountains, where the exiles might have hidden as guerrillas, were eighty miles away.

Castro’s small air force, which the American planes had not destroyed, sank two supply ships carrying ammunition and radios. Castro himself took command of the defense, and his troops, with tanks and artillery, drove the exiles back to the beach. After three days the survivors surrendered. Cuba later traded the prisoners for food and medicine.

WHY KHRUSHCHEV SENT THE MISSILES

In the spring of 1962, Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchevnih-KEE-tuh KROOSH-chefNikita Khrushchev was the Soviet leader who placed missiles in Cuba and then withdrew them. Read more below. made a bold choice. He would secretly place nuclear missiles in Cuba. He had several reasons. One was to defend Castro, whom he expected the Americans to attack again. Another was that the United States had many more long-range nuclear weapons than the Soviet Union did, and its missiles in Turkey and Italy could reach Soviet cities within minutes. Missiles in Cuba would redress the balance. Khrushchev was gambling that he could finish the work before the Americans found out, and then bargain from a position of strength.

The Soviets sent about forty thousand troops, along with missiles, bombers, and even short-range nuclear weapons for use against an invasion. The missilesbuh-LIS-tik MIS-ulA rocket weapon that is launched high into the air and then falls onto a target far away. See the glossary box. of the kind installed there could reach Washington and most of the eastern United States.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Nikita Khrushchev
nih-KEE-tuh KROOSH-chef

Nikita Khrushchev (1894 to 1971) was born to a peasant family in Russia and rose through the Communist Party under Stalin. After Stalin’s death in 1953 he won the struggle for power. In 1956 he stunned the Party by denouncing Stalin’s crimes in a secret speech.

Khrushchev was a bold and restless man who liked surprising his enemies. He pushed the Soviet space program, boasted that he would bury the West, and took chances. The Cuban gamble helped his enemies at home to remove him in October 1964. He spent his last years under watch in a country house, writing memoirs.

THIRTEEN DAYS IN OCTOBER

On October 14, 1962, an American U-2 spy plane photographed a missile site under construction in Cuba. On October 16 the pictures were shown to President KennedyJON eff KEN-uh-deeJohn F. Kennedy was the American president who faced the Soviet missiles in Cuba in October 1962. Read more below.. He formed a committee of about a dozen advisers, called the ExComm, and for a week they met in secret. Some, including the air force chief, General Curtis LeMay, urged an air attack followed by an invasion. Others feared that this could lead to war. Neither side knew then that Soviet troops in Cuba had short-range nuclear weapons and might have used them against an invasion. Kennedy chose a middle course. He would stop Soviet ships from bringing in more weapons, and he would demand that the missiles be removed.

He called it a quarantineKWOR-un-teenIn this lesson, a ring of ships that stops and searches vessels bound for a certain place. Kennedy chose the word instead of blockade. See the glossary box. rather than a blockade, because a blockade is an act of war under international law. On October 22 he told the nation of the missiles and of his demand, in a speech you will read this week. His speech was also an ultimatumul-tih-MAY-tumA final demand that carries a threat of force if it is not met. See the glossary box.: any missile fired from Cuba would be treated as a Soviet attack on the United States. American forces were put on alert, bombers were sent aloft, and the world waited. On October 24 the quarantine line took effect. Soviet ships approached it, and then most stopped or turned back. Secretary of State Dean Rusk said, “We’re eyeball to eyeball, and I think the other fellow just blinked.” The ships carrying missiles had turned back, but the missiles already in Cuba were still being made ready.

On Friday, October 26, a long and emotional letter came from Khrushchev, which you will also read. He offered to remove the missiles if the United States promised not to invade Cuba. On Saturday, October 27, a second and harder letter arrived, which demanded that the United States also remove its missiles from Turkey. That same day an American U-2 was shot down over Cuba and its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson, was killed. Many of Kennedy’s advisers urged him to strike. Out at sea an American destroyer dropped practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine to force it to surface. The submarine’s officers, cut off and short of air, thought that war had begun, and they considered firing a nuclear torpedo. It is now known that one officer, Vasili Arkhipov, refused to agree.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
John F. Kennedy
JON eff KEN-uh-dee

John F. Kennedy (1917 to 1963) was born to a wealthy Massachusetts family and commanded a patrol boat in the Pacific in the Second World War. He wrote a prize-winning book, Profiles in Courage, while a senator. In 1960 he became, at forty-three, the youngest man ever elected president. He won by a narrow margin over Richard Nixon, and he was the first Roman Catholic to hold the office.

His first year was marked by the failure at the Bay of Pigs and a harsh meeting with Khrushchev. In the Cuban crisis he showed both firmness and restraint, and he refused to be rushed. He was assassinated in Dallas on November 22, 1963, only thirteen months after the crisis.

THE DEAL, AND WHO BLINKED

Kennedy answered the first letter and ignored the second. He promised not to invade Cuba if the missiles were removed. At the same time, his brother Robert, the attorney general, told the Soviet ambassador in secret that the American missiles in Turkey would also be removed within a few months, though not as part of a public bargain. On October 28, Khrushchev announced that the missiles would be taken out. The Jupiter missiles left Turkey in the spring of 1963, and for many years the public did not know the secret part of the deal.

So who blinked? Americans long said that Khrushchev did, since he took out the missiles in public and the Jupiters were removed quietly. Later historians point out that both leaders gave ground, and that the trade was real. The better question may be why the world survived. Part of the answer lies in good judgment: Kennedy took time, avoided the most violent choices, and gave Khrushchev a way out. Part of it was luck, such as a submarine officer’s refusal. Both leaders had also seen that war with nuclear weapons could bring ruin to everyone, a fear that held both back. You may wish to ask what would have happened if LeMay’s advice had been taken.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Medium-Range Ballistic Missiles

A ballistic missile is a rocket that burns its fuel in the first minutes of flight and then coasts on a curved path to its target, like a thrown stone. It cannot be recalled or steered after launch, and it gives almost no warning. The missiles sent to Cuba were of two kinds. One, called the R-12, could fly about twelve hundred miles. The other, the R-14, could fly about twenty-eight hundred miles.

Each carried a nuclear warhead many times stronger than the bomb dropped on Hiroshima. At the range of Cuba, such weapons could hit Washington within a few minutes, and the United States could not have stopped them. This speed is why the discovery caused such alarm, and why Kennedy and Khrushchev felt that they had little time to think.

AFTER THE CRISIS

Both leaders had practiced brinkmanshipBRINK-mun-shipThe practice of pushing a dangerous quarrel to the edge of war in the hope that the other side will give way first. See the glossary box., and both had looked into the abyss and did not like what they saw. In June 1963 a direct telephone line, the “hot line,” was set up between Washington and Moscow. In August the two powers and Britain signed a treaty that banned nuclear tests in the air, under the sea, and in space. Khrushchev lost his post in October 1964, partly because of the crisis. Castro stayed in power, and Cuba stayed Communist. The United States kept its embargo, and it still stands.
