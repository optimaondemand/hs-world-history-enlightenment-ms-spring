TODAY'S FOCUS

Italy fought on the winning side in the Great War. Yet within four years a former newspaper editor was its prime minister, and within seven he was on his way to being its dictator. He called his movement fascism. This lesson asks how a country with a parliament and a king gave way to him, and what fascism claimed to be. The question matters, because other nations would soon follow Italy’s example.

A VICTORY THAT FELT LIKE DEFEAT

Italy had joined the Allies in 1915 after they promised it land. It lost about 600,000 men. At the peace conference it received less than it had been promised, and many Italians called their victory “mutilated.” In September 1919 the poet Gabriele D’Annunzio led a band of veterans into the port city of Fiume and held it for fifteen months. He gave balcony speeches to cheering crowds, and his followers wore black shirts and raised their arms in a salute. The show had little effect on the peace, but it showed how many angry veterans would follow a bold leader.

Italy’s government was weak. Parliament was split into many parties, and coalitions fell apart again and again. Prices were rising, and jobs were scarce. In 1919 and 1920 workers went on strike, and some occupied factories while farm laborers seized land. Many property owners and middle-class families feared that Italy would follow Russia into a Bolshevik revolution. They wanted order, and the parliament did not seem able to supply it.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Benito Mussolini
beh-NEE-toh moo-soh-LEE-nee

Benito Mussolini (1883 to 1945) was a gifted speaker and a skillful newspaper editor. He understood crowds, and he liked symbols: the black shirt, the Roman salute, and the balcony. In October 1922 he told a crowd, “Our myth is the nation.”

He became prime minister in October 1922 at the age of thirty-nine. Over the next three years he turned the office into a dictatorship. His Italy was proud and noisy, and it was ruled by fear of his secret police and by his control of the press. He ended the quarrel between Italy and the Catholic Church in 1929. In the 1930s he invaded Ethiopia and allied himself with Hitler. He was overthrown in 1943 and killed by Italian partisans in 1945.

THE MARCH ON ROME

In the elections of 1921 the Fascists won only 35 of 535 seats. But the squads were growing, and in October 1922 Mussolini decided to act. He announced a march on Rome. On October 27 and 28, perhaps thirty thousand Blackshirts gathered near the capital. Many were poorly armed and drenched by rain. The regular army in Rome was larger and better equipped.

The prime minister, Luigi Facta, asked King Victor Emmanuel III to sign a decree of martial law. At first the King agreed. Then he changed his mind. Victor EmmanuelVIK-ter eh-MAN-yoo-ul the THIRDVictor Emmanuel III was the Italian king whose refusal to stop the Fascists in 1922 let Mussolini take power. Read more below. feared a civil war and had little confidence in his own government. Instead he invited Mussolini to form a cabinet. Mussolini traveled to Rome by train from Milan. The “march” became a victory parade after the fact.

Think about how little was needed to stop it. A king who held his nerve could have used the army. The fall of Italian democracy was not fated. It happened because the man whose duty it was to defend the constitution chose not to.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Victor Emmanuel III
VIK-ter eh-MAN-yoo-ul the THIRD

Victor Emmanuel III (1869 to 1947) became king of Italy in 1900 after his father was assassinated. He was a shy, short man who preferred coins and books to politics. Under the Italian constitution he had the power to appoint the prime minister and to command the army.

In October 1922 he faced the decision that defined his reign. His advisers disagreed. Some feared the army might not obey if ordered to fire on the Fascists. He refused to sign the martial law decree and appointed Mussolini instead.

He kept the throne under the dictatorship, but he never regained real power. In 1943 he finally dismissed Mussolini. After the war many Italians blamed him. In 1946 he handed the crown to his son, and soon afterward Italy voted to become a republic.

BUILDING A DICTATORSHIP

At first Mussolini governed with a coalition. He then set out to make himself unbeatable. In 1923 his party passed a law that gave two-thirds of the seats in parliament to any party that won the most votes. In the election of April 1924 the Fascists used threats and violence and won a huge majority.

In May a Socialist deputy named Giacomo Matteotti stood up in parliament and described the violence and fraud. On June 10 Fascists kidnapped and killed him. The opposition walked out in protest and hoped the King would act. He did not. In January 1925 Mussolini stood in parliament and took responsibility for what had happened. Then he dared anyone to challenge him.

Over the next two years opposition parties were banned, newspapers were censored, and a secret police watched citizens. In 1929 Mussolini signed a treaty with the pope that created the Vatican City and won Catholic goodwill. Foreign visitors admired him. Winston Churchill praised him in 1927 for defeating the Communist threat. Few abroad saw what the daily cost of his rule was.

WHAT FASCISM CLAIMED TO BE

Fascism was not a careful philosophy. It was a mood and a method. It praised the nation and the state above the individual. It praised one leader who spoke for all. It despised parliaments as talkers and socialists as enemies. It praised violence as a sign of courage.

Notice what made it appealing. It promised order to people who feared disorder, and glory to people who felt humiliated. It offered unity to a nation divided by class and by party. A free people must handle disorder through law and through habits of trust. Italy’s young democracy had not yet built these habits. When the pressure came, many Italians preferred a strong man to a weak parliament.
