TODAY'S FOCUS

In the early months of 1933 two democracies faced the same economic disaster. In Germany, a new chancellor named Adolf Hitler began to destroy the republic. In the United States, a new president named Franklin Roosevelt began a program of emergency action. Both nations had suffered, but their paths soon parted. This lesson asks why. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: why did some democracies bend in the Depression and others break?

A REPUBLIC BORN IN DEFEAT

Germany’s new republic was born in defeat. In 1919 an assembly meeting in the city of Weimar wrote it a constitution, and the republic took its name from the city. Friedrich Ebert, a Socialist, became its first president. The constitution gave Germans a parliament, the ReichstagRYKS-tahgThe German parliament. The word means ‘the imperial assembly.’ See the glossary box., chosen by a system in which every party won seats in proportion to its votes. That produced many small parties and shaky coalitions. Article 48 let the president rule by emergency decree in a crisis.

The new republic had enemies on both sides. Communists tried to seize power in 1919. Army officers tried in 1920. Radical nationalists murdered several leaders, including the foreign minister Walther Rathenau in 1922. Many Germans blamed the republic for the Versailles treaty, though it was the old Empire that had lost the war. Another legend, that the army had been “stabbed in the back” by traitors at home, made things worse.

1923: THE YEAR THE MONEY DIED

In January 1923 Germany fell behind on reparations payments, and French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, Germany’s richest industrial region. The German government told workers to resist by not working, and it printed money to pay them. The result was hyperinflationhy-per-in-FLAY-shunA fast and extreme rise in prices, so that money loses almost all its value. See the glossary box.. Prices doubled in days, then in hours. By November 1923 one American dollar was worth more than four trillion marks. People carried their wages home in wheelbarrows. A loaf of bread cost hundreds of billions of marks.

Those who owed money paid it off with worthless paper. But those who had saved all their lives lost everything. Retired teachers, clerks, and shopkeepers watched their savings vanish. These middle-class families had once been the republic’s steadiest support. Now many of them felt that the republic, and the whole system of money and law, had betrayed them.

A new chancellor, Gustav StresemannGOOS-tahf SHTRAY-zeh-mahnGustav Stresemann was the German statesman who ended the crisis of 1923 and brought Germany back into European diplomacy. Read more below., called off the resistance and introduced a new currency. The crisis passed. But the memory of it did not.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Gustav Stresemann
GOOS-tahf SHTRAY-zeh-mahn

Gustav Stresemann (1878 to 1929) was a German politician who had been a monarchist and had doubted the new republic in 1919. Over time he came to accept it. As chancellor in the autumn of 1923 he called off the Ruhr resistance and stabilized the currency. He then served as foreign minister from 1923 until his death.

He worked to make Germany a respected member of Europe. He helped to negotiate the Dawes Plan, which lowered reparations payments, and the Locarno treaties of 1925, in which Germany accepted its western borders. Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926. Stresemann shared the Nobel Peace Prize in 1926.

He died on October 3, 1929, only three weeks before the crash on Wall Street. Historians wonder what he might have done in the Depression if he had lived.

THE GOLDEN YEARS AND THE CRASH

After 1924 Germany enjoyed a few good years. American banks lent money to German cities and companies, and Germany used those loans to pay reparations to France and Britain, which in turn paid their war debts to the United States. Money went round in a circle. Factories reopened, and cultural life flourished in Berlin. But the prosperity stood on borrowed money.

In the United States the 1920s brought cars, radios, and rising stock prices. Many people bought stocks with borrowed money, hoping prices would keep rising. On October 24, 1929, prices broke. On October 29 they collapsed. Banks that had lent money to stock buyers failed, and thousands of banks closed over the next few years. Factories shut. By 1932 about one American worker in four had no job.

The crisis spread around the world. American banks called in their loans from Germany, and the circle of money broke. Trade shrank, and countries raised tariffs, which hurt trade further. By 1932 Germany had about six million unemployed. This was a depressiondih-PRESH-unA long and severe slump in business, with high unemployment. See the glossary box., not a passing slump. Britain and France suffered too. Britain left the gold standard in 1931, and both nations kept their parliaments. Nations that had hoped for peace and plenty now turned inward.

TWO ANSWERS

In the United States, President Herbert Hoover tried limited measures, and many Americans thought they were not enough. In November 1932 voters chose Franklin Roosevelt by a wide margin. In his first weeks Roosevelt closed the banks for a few days to stop panic. Congress passed a flood of laws in a hundred days, including bank reforms, farm programs, jobs for young men in the forests, and reliefrih-LEEFMoney, food, or work that a government gives to people in need. See the glossary box. for the poor. Roosevelt talked to the nation on the radio and told people what he was doing. You will read his first inaugural address as this week’s second primary source.

Roosevelt asked for strong powers, but he worked through Congress. When the Supreme Court struck down some of his programs, he complained loudly. Yet he had to live with its decisions, and he faced voters again in 1936, 1940, and 1944. American democracy bent. It did not break.

In Germany the path was different. In 1930 the Chancellor, Heinrich Brüning, lost a vote in the Reichstag and began to govern by emergency decree. He cut government spending, which made unemployment worse. Voters turned to extremes. The Nazi party, which had won under 3 percent of the vote in 1928, won 18 percent in 1930 and 37 percent in July 1932. In Italy, Mussolini, the DuceDOO-chayThe Italian word for leader. Mussolini took this title. See the glossary box., offered one model of a strong state. Many Germans began to look for a strong leader of their own.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Radio and the Fireside Chat

By the early 1930s most American families owned a radio. Franklin Roosevelt used it with great skill. A few days after taking office, on March 12, 1933, he gave the first of his “fireside chats,” a series of calm talks that explained his policies in plain words. In the first, he told Americans how banks worked and why it was safe to put their money back.

The chats helped to restore confidence. They also showed how a leader in a democracy could use a new technology to speak directly to citizens.

Dictators used radio too. Hitler’s government spread cheap radio sets so that more Germans could hear its speeches. Technology could serve a free people or a regime that wanted to control them.

WHY DID SOME BEND AND OTHERS BREAK?

Historians give several answers. Germany’s suffering was deeper, and it came on top of the hyperinflation that had already wounded the middle class. Its republic was young and unloved, and many Germans had never felt loyal to it. Its voting system produced weak governments. Article 48 gave the president a tool that could be used against the constitution. Britain and France, also hit hard, kept their democracies, though both faced strong parties at the extremes. America’s institutions were older, and its people had long habits of self-government. Its parties and its courts could absorb the shock.

Notice that none of these answers makes the outcome certain. Stresemann’s death came at the worst moment. Leaders in Germany made choices that could have been different. A republic is kept alive by the habits and loyalty of its people. When that loyalty is gone, no clause in a constitution can replace it.
