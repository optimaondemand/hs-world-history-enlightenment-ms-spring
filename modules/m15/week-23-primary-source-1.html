PRIMARY SOURCE 1
Mussolini: “The Doctrine of Fascism” (1932)
The Fascist regime explains, in its own words, what it believes.
💡 BEFORE YOU READ

In 1932 the Italian government published a long article on fascism in a national encyclopedia. It was signed by Benito Mussolini. Modern scholars say the first part, on the fundamental ideas, was written by the philosopher Giovanni Gentile, one of the regime’s leading thinkers. Mussolini then added a second part on the political and social doctrine. Together they are the closest thing fascism has to an official statement of belief.

A few terms may be new. “Liberalism” here means the belief in individual rights and limited government. “Absolutism” means rule by a king with unlimited power. “Pacifism” means the belief that war is wrong. Mussolini also uses the word “totalitarian,” which you met in the lesson. Fascists used it as a boast, not as a complaint.

Read this as a historian should. Ask who wrote it and for what audience, what it says the state should be and what it says about the individual, and what it says about war and peace.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
The Doctrine of Fascism
Benito Mussolini (with Giovanni Gentile), 1932. English text from the official translation in Fascism: Doctrine and Institutions (Rome: Ardita, 1935), as reproduced online. Footnote numbers removed, and a few obvious scanning errors in the online copy corrected. [...] marks a cut.

Anti-individualistic, the Fascist conception of life stresses the importance of the State and accepts the individual only in so far as his interests coincide with those of the State, which stands for the conscience and the universal, will of man as a historic entity. It is opposed to classical liberalism which arose as a reaction to absolutism and exhausted its historical function when the State became the expression of the conscience and will of the people. Liberalism denied the State in the name of the individual; Fascism reasserts the rights of the State as expressing the real essence of the individual.

The Fascist conception of the State is all embracing; outside of it no human or spiritual values can exist, much less have value. Thus understood, Fascism, is totalitarian, and the Fascist State - a synthesis and a unit inclusive of all values - interprets, develops, and potentates the whole life of a people.

No individuals or groups (political parties, cultural associations, economic unions, social classes) outside the State.

[...]

Fascism does not, generally speaking, believe in the possibility or utility of perpetual peace. It therefore discards pacifism as a cloak for cowardly supine renunciation in contradistinction to self-sacrifice. War alone keys up all human energies to their maximum tension and sets the seal of nobility on those peoples who have the courage to face it.

[...]

A party governing a nation “totalitarianly” is a new departure in history. There are no points of reference nor of comparison.

[...]

Political doctrines pass; nations remain. We are free to believe that this is the century of authority, a century tending to the “right”, a Fascist century. If the XIXth century was the century of the individual (liberalism implies individualism) we are free to believe that this is the “collective” century, and therefore the century of the State.

Notice where the word “totalitarian” appears and how Mussolini uses it. Notice also what the passage says about groups outside the state, such as parties and unions. You can return to this page as often as you like.
