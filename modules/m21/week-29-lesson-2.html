TODAY'S FOCUS

After Pearl Harbor Japan won everywhere it struck. Then in six months it lost the heart of its fleet. This lesson follows the Pacific war from the Philippines to Midway and beyond. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: was Midway luck or skill? Look for each place where the outcome could have gone the other way.

SIX MONTHS OF VICTORIES

Japan’s plan worked at first. In the Philippines the American and Filipino defenders held out on the Bataan peninsula until April 1942, and the last island fortress, Corregidor, fell in May. Thousands of prisoners died on the march and in the camps that followed. In Malaya, British forces were outfought, and on February 15 Singapore surrendered, with about 80,000 men, the biggest surrender in British history. The Dutch East Indies fell, and Japan won the oil it had come for. By May its armies stood on the border of India.

The United States struck back once, for the sake of morale. On April 18, sixteen army bombers under Colonel James Doolittle flew from the carrier Hornet to bomb Tokyo. They did little damage, and the crews had to crash-land in China. But the raid shocked Japan, because the homeland had been thought safe. It also made the navy anxious to destroy the carriers it had missed at Pearl Harbor.

CORAL SEA AND THE CODE

In early May the Japanese sent a force toward Port Moresby, in New Guinea. American carriersAIR-kraft KAIR-ee-erA warship with a long flat deck from which airplanes take off and land. See the glossary box. met it in the Coral Sea. It was the first carrier battle in history in which the two fleets never saw each other, because the planes did all the fighting. The United States lost the carrier Lexington, and Japan lost a light carrier, had a second damaged, and lost most of the planes of a third. The Japanese turned back from Port Moresby. The damage to those two carriers kept them out of the next battle.

Meanwhile a small group of Americans in the basement of the Pearl Harbor naval base was reading some of Japan’s secret messages. Their chief, Commander Joseph Rochefort, could read enough of the navy’s main code to see that a big operation was planned against a target called “AF.” He believed AF was Midway, a tiny island about 1,300 miles northwest of Hawaii. Officers in Washington thought it was somewhere else. To prove his point Rochefort had Midway send a plain-language message, saying that its water plant had broken. Soon a Japanese message reported that AF was short of water.

Nimitz moved his three carriers to wait, and he ordered the damaged carrier Yorktown to be patched in three days, not the three months the yard had estimated. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Chester Nimitz
CHES-ter NIM-its

Chester Nimitz (1885 to 1966) grew up in the Texas hill country and entered the Naval Academy at seventeen. He became an expert on submarines and engines. After Pearl Harbor, Roosevelt chose him to lead the shattered Pacific Fleet, and he arrived in Hawaii on Christmas Day, 1941.

He was a calm and fair man who listened to his officers and then made the decision himself. Before Midway he risked his fleet on the word of his codebreakers. Later he directed the whole island campaign, from the ships of his fleet to the supply lines that fed them across thousands of miles of ocean. In September 1945 he signed the Japanese surrender on behalf of the United States.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Breaking JN-25

JN-25 was a two-step code. First, each word or phrase was replaced with a five-digit number from a code book of tens of thousands of entries. Then a second set of random numbers was added to disguise the message. To read it, a codebreaker had to find both the book and the added numbers.

The Americans worked with punched cards, early machines, and a great deal of pencil work. They never read every message, and the book changed often. In the weeks before Midway they could read perhaps a tenth or a fifth of what they caught. Even small pieces were enough, such as the name of a target, the date, and the ships that would take part. The codebreakers also learned which message pieces mattered, because Japan repeated its stock phrases over and over.

JUNE 4, 1942

Yamamoto’s plan was complicated. A decoy force would strike the Aleutian Islands, and the Kidô ButaiKEE-doh BOO-tieA Japanese term for mobile force. It names the strike force of aircraft carriers that attacked Pearl Harbor. See the glossary box., with four carriers, would hit Midway. He expected the American fleet to hurry out, and his battleships would be waiting to destroy it. Instead, three American carriers sat northeast of Midway, unseen. Japan had four carriers there, and the United States had three, plus the airfield on the island.

Early on June 4, Admiral Nagumo sent his first strike against Midway. A search plane from one cruiser was late taking off, and it found the American ships about half an hour after it should have. Nagumo had held back half his planes armed with torpedoes in case ships appeared. He changed their weapons to bombs for another strike on the island, then had to change them back. His decks were crowded with planes, fuel hoses, and bombs.

The American attack began badly. The slow torpedo planes flew in without cover and in separate waves. Nearly all were shot down, and one squadron lost every plane but one. They scored no hits. But their attacks drew the Japanese fighters down to the water. At 10:22 dive bombers from the carriers Enterprise and Yorktown arrived above the Japanese fleet unopposed. One group had found the fleet only because its leader, Lieutenant Commander Clarence McClusky, followed the wake of a lone Japanese destroyer. Within five minutes three carriers, Akagi, Kaga, and Soryu, were burning.

The fourth carrier, Hiryu, struck back and wrecked the Yorktown. That afternoon American dive bombers destroyed Hiryu. That night Admiral Raymond Spruance, who commanded the two other carriers, turned east instead of chasing the Japanese in the dark, where he might have met their battleships. Some criticized him, but his caution saved his fleet. Yamamoto called off the attack. On June 7 a Japanese submarine finished off Yorktown.

🛡 BATTLE
The Battle of Midway

Midway is a pair of tiny islands in the middle of the Pacific, about 1,300 miles from Hawaii. In 1942 it held an American airfield and a small garrison. Japan wanted to take it and lure the American carriers into a trap. The island itself rose only a few feet above the sea. Its Marine pilots flew some old and slow aircraft against the first Japanese raid, and many did not return.

Japan lost four aircraft carriers, a heavy cruiser, about 250 aircraft, and some 3,000 men, many of them its most skilled pilots and mechanics. The United States lost the carrier Yorktown, a destroyer, and about 150 aircraft, with about 300 men killed. The Japanese navy never again had the strength to take the lead in the Pacific.

LUCK OR SKILL?

List what the Americans did on purpose. They read the code, they trusted the reading, they repaired the Yorktown, and they placed their carriers where the enemy would not look. Then list what happened by chance. A search plane started late. A destroyer happened to show a dive-bomber leader where to look. Torpedo planes that failed pulled the enemy fighters low just as the dive bombers came. The dive bombers arrived when Japanese decks were full of planes, fuel, and bombs. Notice which items on your list depend on one another, and decide how much each weighs. You will need your list for the discussion.

THE LONG ROAD

Midway did not end the war. In August 1942 American Marines landed on Guadalcanal in the Solomon Islands to stop Japan from building an airfield there. The fight lasted six months and cost both sides ships, planes, and men, and Japan left in February 1943.
