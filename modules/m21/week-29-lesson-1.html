TODAY'S FOCUS

By the end of 1941 Germany ruled most of Europe and Japan was at war in China. Neither war had yet brought in the United States. Then in one December morning both wars joined into one. This lesson asks why Japan chose to strike a nation far larger than itself. It also prepares you for the Pacific war you will study in the next lesson.

OIL AND THE EMBARGO

Japan had been at war in China since 1937, and the fight swallowed men and supplies. The country has almost no oil of its own, and about four-fifths of its oil came from the United States. In September 1940 Japan joined Germany and Italy in the AxisAK-sisThe alliance of Germany, Italy, and Japan in the Second World War. See the glossary box.. That same month, with France beaten, its troops moved into the northern part of French Indochina, in what is now Vietnam. Britain and France held rich colonies in Southeast Asia, and Japan began to eye them as well. Its eyes were on the oil of the Dutch East Indies.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Franklin Roosevelt
FRANK-lin ROH-zuh-velt

Franklin Delano Roosevelt (1882 to 1945) was born to a wealthy family in New York. He rose in politics as a young man, and in 1921 he was struck by polio and lost the use of his legs. He returned to politics anyway and became governor of New York in 1929.

He won the presidency in 1932 and was elected four times, which no one had done before or has done since. He answered the Depression with a set of programs called the New Deal. In the years before Pearl Harbor he argued that America should help the nations that fought the dictators, and he moved the country slowly toward that goal. He died on April 12, 1945, a few weeks before the war in Europe ended.

THE CHOICE IN TOKYO

Japan’s leaders saw three roads. They could give way and pull out of China, which the army would not accept. They could keep talking and hope to bargain. Or they could seize the oil of Southeast Asia by force, which meant war with Britain, the Dutch, and almost certainly the United States. Prime Minister Konoe sought a meeting with Roosevelt, and nothing came of it. In October he resigned, and General Hideki Tojo, the army minister, took his place. On November 26 the American secretary of state, Cordell Hull, sent a note that asked Japan to leave China and Indochina. Japan’s leaders took it as a final demand and decided they would not obey it.

The man who planned the answer was Admiral Isoroku Yamamotoee-soh-ROH-koo yah-mah-MOH-tohIsoroku Yamamoto was the Japanese admiral who planned the attack on Pearl Harbor. Read more below., who led the Combined Fleet. He had lived in America and feared its factories. He told one leader that he would run wild for six months to a year, and that after that he had no confidence. His plan was to cripple the American fleet at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii, so that Japan could take the oil it needed and build a wall of islands that the United States would find too costly to break.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Isoroku Yamamoto
ee-soh-ROH-koo yah-mah-MOH-toh

Isoroku Yamamoto (1884 to 1943) lost two fingers as a young officer at the great naval battle of Tsushima in 1905. He later studied at Harvard and served as a naval attaché in Washington. He knew how much steel and oil America could produce, and he was wary of war against it.

He opposed the pact with Germany and Italy, and he received death threats from men who wanted war. But when the decision was made he threw himself into it, and he put his hopes in the aircraft carrier. He thought that a heavy blow at the start might lead America to settle. In April 1943 American fighters shot down his plane after American codebreakers learned his flight plan. He was killed in the jungle of Bougainville.

THE STRIKE

The strike force was called the Kidô ButaiKEE-doh BOO-tieA Japanese term for mobile force. It names the strike force of aircraft carriers that attacked Pearl Harbor. See the glossary box.. It had six aircraft carriersAIR-kraft KAIR-ee-erA warship with a long flat deck from which airplanes take off and land. See the glossary box. and more than 350 planes under Admiral Chuichi Nagumo. It left the Kuril Islands on November 26, took a long path across the cold North Pacific, and kept radio silence. It sailed away from shipping lanes, and no American ship saw it.

The first planes took off from a point about 230 miles north of Hawaii. They struck at 7:55 on Sunday morning, December 7. Eight American battleships were in the harbor, and every one of them was sunk or damaged. The USS Arizona blew up, and more than 1,100 men died with her. The USS Oklahoma rolled over. Planes lined up wing to wing on the airfields were burned where they stood. In all, 2,403 Americans were killed. The Japanese lost 29 aircraft. A radar post on the island had seen the planes coming, but a young officer took them for American bombers that were expected that morning.

It was a stunning success, but it was incomplete. The American aircraft carriers were away at sea, and so were the fuel tanks and repair yards. Nagumo chose not to send a third wave against them. Within a year the carriers he spared would meet his own. Consider how this might have gone if the carriers had been in port.

WHY AMERICA WAS SURPRISED

The surprise was not total. American codebreakers could read Japan’s diplomatic messages, and they knew by late November that the talks were failing. On November 27 Washington sent its Pacific commanders a war warning. But the warning pointed toward the Philippines and Southeast Asia, where Japan’s troop ships were seen sailing. No one in a position to decide believed that Japan would risk a long voyage across the northern Pacific to strike Hawaii. Intelligence was also scattered among separate offices, and no one gathered the pieces together.

The two commanders in Hawaii, Admiral Husband Kimmel and General Walter Short, were removed from their posts. Many Americans then and since have looked for a hidden plot to explain the disaster. Several official inquiries found none. They found failures of imagination, divided duties, and poor sharing of information. Consider how a free country, long at peace, can fail to see a danger that was plain to a few.

🛡 BATTLE
Pearl Harbor

Pearl Harbor lies on the island of Oahu, in the middle of the Pacific. It was the main base of the American Pacific Fleet. Most of the battleships were moored in pairs along Battleship Row, on the east side of Ford Island, where they were easy to see and could not move quickly.

The attack came in two waves of about two hours. Torpedo planes hit the ships from the water, dive bombers struck from above, and fighters shot up the airfields. Four of the eight battleships were sunk and the other four damaged. The Arizona and the Oklahoma never sailed again, but the rest were raised and repaired, and most fought again. The USS Arizona lies where she sank, and a memorial now stands above her.

ONE WAR, NOT TWO

The same day Japan also struck Malaya, Hong Kong, Guam, Wake Island, and the Philippines. On December 8 Roosevelt asked Congress for a declaration of war. The Senate voted 82 to 0, and the House voted 388 to 1. Roosevelt called December 7 “a date which will live in infamy,” and you will read the speech this week. A famous line says that Yamamoto feared the attack had awakened a sleeping giant. There is no proof that he said it.

Then came a second surprise. On December 11 Hitler declared war on the United States, and so did Mussolini. The pact with Japan did not require this, because Japan was the one that had attacked. Hitler probably thought that war with America was coming anyway, and that Japan would tie down American forces in the Pacific. Notice what a difference this made. Without it Roosevelt might have found it hard to persuade Americans to fight Germany first, which was the plan his advisers had made.
