TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1914 many people believed the war would be over by Christmas. Within a year they knew better. This lesson asks what happens to a nation when everyone is drawn into a war: soldiers, factory workers, farmers, writers, and schoolchildren. It also asks a question that every free country had to answer. How much freedom may a government take away in the name of victory?

A WAR OF WHOLE PEOPLES

No one had planned for a war like this. The armies of 1914 were huge, and they used up men and shells faster than any government had expected. Soon every nation had to put its whole economy on a war footing.

Britain had no large army in 1914, so it asked for volunteers. Lord Kitchener, the secretary of state for war, called for a new army. A famous poster showed his pointing finger with the words “Your Country Needs You.” About two and a half million men volunteered in the first sixteen months. By early 1916 that was not enough, and Parliament passed a law to draft men into the army. In 1915 a shortage of shells caused a scandal, and a new Ministry of Munitions was created under David Lloyd George. Hundreds of thousands of women went to work in factories and on farms, taking jobs men had left. In 1918 many women over thirty won the vote in Britain. Even children took part. They collected scrap metal, knitted socks for soldiers, and bought war savings stamps with their pennies.

Germany felt the war more sharply. The British navy cut off its trade by sea, and food grew scarce. German officials, led by industrialists such as Walther Rathenau, organized raw materials and factories. But the potato crop failed in 1916, and the winter of 1916 to 1917 became known as the “turnip winter,” because turnips were all many families had to eat. THE BATTLE FOR BELIEF

A government that needs its people’s sons, labor, and savings needs their belief. So each government turned to propagandaprop-uh-GAN-duhInformation, true or false, spread to win support for a cause. The word usually points to messages built to move feelings more than to inform. See the glossary box.: information, true or false, spread to win support. Britain set up a secret propaganda bureau in 1914. In May 1915 it published the Bryce Report, an official study of German conduct in Belgium. German troops had in fact killed thousands of Belgian civilians in the first weeks of the war. They burned the old university library at Louvain. Later historians confirmed the killings. But they also found that some of the most horrible stories in the report, such as tales of mutilated children, rested on weak or no evidence. You will read part of the report in this week’s first primary source.

Other stories had a firmer basis. In October 1915 the Germans shot Edith Cavell, a British nurse in Brussels who had helped Allied soldiers escape. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Edith Cavell
EE-dith KAV-ul

Edith Cavell (1865 to 1915) was an English nurse who ran a training school for nurses in Brussels. After the Germans occupied the city, she helped about two hundred Allied soldiers hide and escape to the neutral Netherlands. German authorities arrested her in August 1915. She admitted what she had done. A German military court sentenced her to death, and she was shot on October 12, 1915.

Her execution caused outrage around the world. German officials said the law of war allowed it. Britain made her a symbol of German cruelty. The chaplain who saw her the night before reported that she said, “Patriotism is not enough. I must have no hatred or bitterness towards anyone.”

Her case shows how a true story could be used as propaganda without becoming false.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
George Creel
JORJ kreel

George Creel (1876 to 1953) was a newspaper writer and reformer from Missouri. In April 1917 President Wilson named him head of the Committee on Public Information, a new government agency. Its job was to explain the war to Americans and to the world.

Creel’s committee printed millions of pamphlets, sent out news stories, made films, and organized thousands of volunteer speakers, called Four Minute Men, who spoke in theaters between films. Creel said he wanted to persuade rather than to censor. Yet the committee also pressed newspapers to follow its guidelines.

After the war, Americans debated whether such an agency belonged in a free country. Creel defended his work, and many people remained uneasy about it.

FREEDOM UNDER STRAIN

Governments also silenced opponents. In Britain a law called the Defence of the Realm Act, passed in August 1914, gave the government wide powers, including control of the press. In the United States, Congress passed the Espionage Act in June 1917 and the Sedition Act in May 1918. These laws made it a crime to interfere with the draft or to speak against the war effort in certain ways. The Post Office refused to carry newspapers it judged disloyal.

Some of the people caught by these laws were real spies and saboteurs. Others were only critics. Eugene Debs, a leader of the Socialist Party, gave a speech against the war in Ohio in June 1918. He was sentenced to ten years in prison. In 1920 he ran for president from his cell and received almost a million votes.

Ordinary Americans with German names also suffered. Schools stopped teaching German. Some towns renamed streets, and sauerkraut became “liberty cabbage.” In April 1918 a mob in Illinois lynched Robert Prager, a German-born man suspected of disloyalty. A jury later acquitted the men accused of killing him.

Free states gave reasons for these limits. A leaflet that told soldiers to refuse orders could cost lives at the front, and a nation at war cannot afford to be divided. Critics answered that a free country that silences its critics has given up the very thing it is defending.

In 1919 the Supreme Court heard a case about a Socialist leaflet. You will read part of its decision in this week’s second primary source.

TWO QUESTIONS FOR A FREE PEOPLE

A republic depends on its citizens’ virtue and on their access to the truth. Both were under pressure. A government that wins support through lies may win the war and lose something else. After 1918, many people said they would never again trust official claims about atrocities. Some historians argue that this distrust made it harder for the world to believe true reports about Nazi crimes in the 1940s.

At the same time, a government that cannot unite its people cannot fight. Hegel said war tests a state’s health, and the test is as much about unity as about weapons. The hard question is where to draw the line. Wartime laws were meant to protect the nation. Did they protect it, or did they threaten the freedom the nation was fighting for?
