TODAY'S FOCUS

The trenches were not the only place the war was fought. Both sides looked for ways around the deadlock. One tried to seize a narrow waterway, one tried to starve its enemy, and one tried to sink every ship that carried food to Britain. This lesson follows these three attempts and asks why each one failed to end the war quickly, and what each cost.

WHY THE STRAITS MATTERED

In late October 1914 the Ottoman Empire joined the war on Germany’s side. Its fleet shelled Russian ports on the Black Sea, and Russia, Britain, and France declared war on it. The Ottoman capital, Constantinople, guarded the straitsSTRAYTSA narrow strip of water that joins two larger bodies of water. See the glossary box. that join the Black Sea to the Mediterranean. They are called the Bosphorus and the Dardanelles.

This mattered enormously for Russia. Much of its grain was sold abroad through the straits, and the guns and shells it needed came in the same way. With the straits closed, Russia was nearly cut off from its allies. Its other ports were far away or frozen for much of the year.

Winston Churchill, the head of the British navy, thought he saw a bold answer. A fleet could force its way through the Dardanelles, reach Constantinople, knock the Ottoman Empire out of the war, and open a sea road to Russia. It was a plan to go around the trenches.

GALLIPOLI

The fleet tried on March 18, 1915. Hidden mines sank three old battleships and crippled others, and the admiral called off the attack. Then the Allies decided to land troops on the Gallipoli peninsula, which lies along the Dardanelles, to destroy the forts from the land.

The landings began on April 25. Soldiers from Australia and New Zealand, called the Anzacs, came ashore at a cove that is still called Anzac Cove. British, French, and other troops landed farther south. The defenders were Ottoman soldiers, with German advisers, and they held the high ground. One of their officers, Mustafa KemalMOOS-tah-FAH keh-MAHLMustafa Kemal was the Ottoman officer who held the high ground at Gallipoli and later founded modern Turkey. Read more below., rushed his division to the key hills.

The campaign became another stalemate. The soldiers fought in heat and crowded trenches, plagued by flies and disease. After eight months the Allies gave up. They withdrew their troops in December 1915 and January 1916 in a careful operation that cost very few lives. Between the two sides, well over four hundred thousand men were killed, wounded, or sick. Churchill had already lost his place at the head of the navy, in May 1915, and the failure damaged his career for years.

Historians still debate whether the navy might have succeeded if it had tried again. Some argue that the Ottoman guns were low on shells, and that a second attack might have broken through. Others say the minefields and forts made success unlikely. What is clear is that the Allies lost the element of surprise, and the Ottoman defenders had time to prepare.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Mustafa Kemal
MOOS-tah-FAH keh-MAHL

Mustafa Kemal (1881 to 1938) was an Ottoman officer who commanded a division at Gallipoli. On the first day of the landings he saw how dangerous the Anzac beachhead was and rushed his men to the hills above it. He is said to have told one regiment, “I do not order you to attack. I order you to die.” Whatever his exact words, his men held the line.

After the war the Ottoman Empire collapsed. Kemal led a Turkish army that drove out foreign forces, and in 1923 he became the first president of the Republic of Turkey. Later the Turkish parliament gave him the name Atatürk, which means “father of the Turks.”

Gallipoli made his reputation. It shows how one commander’s quick judgment on one morning could shape a battle and, later, the history of a nation.

THE WAR AT SEA

Britain ruled the seas. Its navy was the largest in the world, and from the first weeks of the war it used that power to enforce a blockadeblok-AYDUsing warships to cut off an enemy’s trade by sea. See the glossary box. of Germany. Ships heading to German ports were stopped and searched. Food, fuel, and raw materials ran short. The blockade was a form of attrition that worked slowly, by hunger, and the German people felt it most in the winter of 1916 to 1917.

The two great fleets met only once. At Jutland, in the North Sea, on May 31 and June 1, 1916, the British lost more ships and more men than the Germans did. But the German fleet returned to port and never again challenged the blockade in force. A battle that looked like a draw had decided the matter in Britain’s favor.

The blockade also raised hard questions of law. In November 1914 Britain declared the North Sea a military area and mined it, so neutral ships had to pass through British inspection. The United States protested, but its protests were mild next to its protests against Germany. One reason was that the British blockade took cargoes and money, while the U-boats took lives.

Germany needed another answer. Its answer was the submarine.

THE U-BOAT AND THE GAMBLE

In February 1915 Germany declared the waters around Britain a war zone and warned that its U-boatsYOO-bohtShort for the German word Unterseeboot, meaning ‘undersea boat.’ It is the name for a German submarine. See the glossary box. would sink enemy ships there. On May 7, 1915, the submarine U-20 torpedoed the British passenger liner Lusitania off the coast of Ireland. Almost twelve hundred people died, including 128 Americans. The ship also carried rifle cartridges and other war goods, and Germany used that fact to defend itself. But most of the world judged the sinking of a passenger ship a crime.

President Wilson protested firmly. Germany promised to warn passenger ships before attacking, and for a time it kept its word. Then, in January 1917, its leaders made a fateful gamble. They decided to sink every ship that sailed to Britain, with no warning. German naval officers promised that Britain would be starved into peace in about five months, before American troops could arrive in force. Germany announced the new policy to begin on February 1.

The result nearly worked. In April 1917 U-boats sank about 860,000 tons of shipping in a single month. Britain’s food stocks shrank to a dangerous level. But Britain then began to sail its merchant ships in a convoyKON-voyA group of merchant ships that sail together, guarded by warships. See the glossary box., guarded by warships. Losses fell, and the food kept coming.

The gamble failed, and it had a cost. The United States broke off relations with Germany on February 3, 1917. By April it was at war.

🔧 TECHNOLOGY
The U-Boat

The submarine was a new weapon in 1914. German U-boats ran on diesel engines on the surface and on batteries underwater. They carried torpedoes and a deck gun, and they could stay hidden for days. A single U-boat could sink a large ship with one torpedo.

The older rules of naval war said that a warship must stop a merchant ship, search it, and let the crew take to the boats before sinking it. A submarine was small and weak on the surface, so stopping to search a ship put it at risk. That is why Germany ordered its U-boats to attack without warning.

During the war, U-boats sank more than five thousand merchant ships. The convoy system, better depth charges, and new patrols finally cut their success.

🛡 BATTLE
The Gallipoli Campaign

The Gallipoli Campaign lasted from February 1915 to January 1916. It began as a naval attack on the Dardanelles and turned into a land campaign on the Gallipoli peninsula. British, French, Australian, New Zealand, and other Allied troops fought against Ottoman defenders who held the hills above the beaches.

The fighting was fierce and the conditions were harsh. Soldiers suffered from heat, thirst, flies, and disease. Neither side could break through. In December 1915 and January 1916 the Allies withdrew in a well-planned retreat.

For Australia and New Zealand, the landing on April 25 became a day of national memory, called Anzac Day. For Turkey, it was the first step in the career of the man who would lead its republic.

WHAT THE SEA WAR SHOWED

Neither side obeyed the old rules of sea warfare for long. Britain stretched the law to blockade neutral trade, and Germany broke it to sink ships without warning. Each said the enemy had started it. But the U-boat campaign did something the blockade did not. It threatened the lives of neutral citizens, including Americans. Next week’s lesson shows how that choice helped bring the United States into the war.

The sea war also showed how much a trading nation depends on others. Britain’s survival depended on food from North America, Argentina, and Australia, carried across thousands of miles of ocean. A country that lives by trade can be strangled at sea.

The sea war also leaves a lesson about plans. Churchill’s bold attack and Germany’s submarine gamble were both attempts to win quickly. Both failed, and each cost the nation that tried it dearly.
