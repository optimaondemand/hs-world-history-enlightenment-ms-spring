PRIMARY SOURCE 1
The Bryce Report on Alleged German Outrages (1915)
A British committee reports on the conduct of German troops in Belgium and France.
💡 BEFORE YOU READ

In 1914 the British government asked a committee of distinguished men to study reports of German conduct in Belgium and France. The chairman was Viscount Bryce, a respected historian, diplomat, and former ambassador to the United States. The committee published its report on May 12, 1915.

The report was read around the world and used heavily in British and American propaganda. It was based on depositions, which are written statements by witnesses. The statements were taken by lawyers in Britain, and the witnesses did not testify under oath.

Later historians, studying German and Belgian records, confirmed that German troops killed thousands of civilians in 1914. But they also concluded that many of the most shocking individual stories in the report were not supported by evidence.

Read this as a historian should. Ask who wrote it, what kind of evidence it rests on, and what the committee wanted its readers to do after reading it.

📜 PRIMARY SOURCE
Report of the Committee on Alleged German Outrages
Presented to the British government, 1915; signed by Viscount Bryce and six other committee members. Excerpts from the introduction and conclusions, text as transcribed by the Avalon Project, Yale Law School. [...] marks a cut. The online transcription has small errors, so the excerpt avoids the one garbled sentence in the introduction.

More than 1,200 depositions made by these witnesses have been submitted to and considered by the Committee.

The depositions were in all cases taken down in this country by gentlemen of legal knowledge and experience, though, of course, they had no authority to administer an oath. They were instructed not to “lead” the witnesses, or make any suggestions to them, and also to impress upon them the necessity for care and precision in giving their evidence.

[...]

Many hesitated to speak lest what they said, if it should ever be published, might involve their friends or relatives at home in danger, and it was found necessary to give an absolute promise that names should not be disclosed.

For this reason names have been omitted.

[...]

CONCLUSIONS.

From the foregoing pages it will be seen that the Committee have come to a definite conclusion upon each of the heads under which the evidence has been classified.

It is proved--

(i) That there were in many parts of Belgium deliberate and systematically organised massacres of the civil population, accompanied by many isolated murders and other outrages.

(ii) That in the conduct of the war generally innocent civilians, both men and women, were murdered in large numbers, women violated, and children murdered.

[...]

Sensible as they are of the gravity of these conclusions, the Committee conceive that they would be doing less than their duty if they failed to record them as fully established by the evidence. Murder, lust, and pillage prevailed over many parts of Belgium on a scale unparalleled in any war between civilised nations during the last three centuries.

Our function is ended when we have stated what the evidence establishes, but we may be permitted to express our belief that these disclosures will not have been made in vain if they touch and rouse the conscience of mankind, and we venture to hope that as soon as the present war is over, the nations of the world in council will consider what means can be provided and sanctions devised to prevent the recurrence of such horrors as our generation is now witnessing.

📌 A NOTE FOR YOU

Notice the sentence that says names have been omitted, and notice the reason given. Think about what that choice does for the witnesses and what it does for a reader who wants to check the stories. Then compare the committee’s stated purpose in its last paragraph with the way governments used the report. You can return to this page as often as you like.
