TODAY'S FOCUS

In 1939 a few European nations ruled much of Asia and Africa. By 1975 almost all of those empires were gone. This lesson looks at two cases that ended very differently: India, which won independence from Britain in 1947, and Algeria, which won it from France in 1962. As you read, keep one question in mind for this week’s discussion: were the rights of man a gift of empire or a weapon against it? THE WAR THAT WEAKENED EMPIRES

The Second World War shook the empires of Europe. Britain was nearly bankrupt, France had been conquered and then liberated, and Holland had lost its rich colonies to Japan. The Japanese had shown that a European army could be beaten by an Asian one. The two superpowersSOO-per-pow-erA nation so strong in arms and wealth that it can shape events around the world. See the glossary box. were also against empires, though for different reasons. The Americans remembered that they had been a colony once, and the Soviets called empire a crime of capitalism. In 1941 Roosevelt and Churchill signed the Atlantic Charter, which promised that peoples would be able to choose their own governments. Churchill later said that this applied to the conquered nations of Europe and not to the British Empire. Many people in Asia and Africa read it differently. In the Second World War the Congress refused to back Britain’s war effort without a promise of freedom, and in 1942 it called on the British to Quit India. Gandhi and the other leaders were jailed.

A second force was growing. Many of India’s Muslims, about a quarter of the people, feared that they would be a permanent minority in a Hindu-led nation. Their leader, Muhammad Ali Jinnahmoo-HAM-id AH-lee JIN-ahMuhammad Ali Jinnah was the leader of the Muslim League who won the creation of Pakistan. Read more below., called for a separate Muslim state, Pakistan. Talks failed. In August 1946 riots in Calcutta killed thousands, and the killing spread. Britain’s new Labour government, worn out by the war, decided to leave. It sent Lord Mountbatten to arrange the handover, and he moved the date forward to August 1947. A British judge, Cyril Radcliffe, who had never been to India, was given about five weeks to draw the new borders through Punjab and Bengal.

India and Pakistan became independent on August 14 and 15, 1947. Roughly ten to fifteen million people crossed the new border, Hindus and Sikhs one way and Muslims the other, in one of the largest forced migrations in history. Estimates of the dead run from two hundred thousand to two million. The partitionpar-TISH-unThe division of a country or territory into two or more separate parts. See the glossary box. left a quarrel over Kashmir that still divides the two nations. Gandhi, who had opposed partition, was shot by a Hindu nationalist in January 1948. India kept the parliament, courts, and army that Britain had built. It adopted a constitution in 1950 and became the largest democracy in the world. Pakistan, meanwhile, was ruled for much of its history by generals. Notice how much the outcome depended on a few men, and on a rushed schedule.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Mohandas Gandhi
moh-HAHN-dus GAHN-dee

Mohandas Gandhi (1869 to 1948) trained as a lawyer in London and spent twenty years in South Africa, where he first used peaceful mass protest against unjust laws. Returning to India in 1915, he became the leading figure in the Congress. He was a devout Hindu who respected other faiths. He wore homespun cloth, lived simply, and fasted to move the conscience of others.

His greatest campaigns, such as the Salt March, drew millions into peaceful protest and shamed the British government before the world. He could not, however, keep India united, and the violence of 1947 broke his heart. He was assassinated in New Delhi on January 30, 1948.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Muhammad Ali Jinnah
moo-HAM-id AH-lee JIN-ah

Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876 to 1948) was a lawyer, educated in London, who began his career as a member of the Indian National Congress. In the 1930s he left the Congress and became the head of the Muslim League. He argued that Muslims were a nation of their own and could not be safe under a Hindu majority.

His determination and skill as an advocate won Pakistan in only seven years. He became its first governor-general, but he was already gravely ill with tuberculosis, which he had hidden, and he died in September 1948. Some historians think that he never meant the demand for Pakistan to go so far, while others think he always meant it.

ALGERIA: THE WAR FRANCE COULD NOT END

France had conquered Algeria in 1830 and had made it part of France itself, with three departments governed from Paris. By 1954 about a million Europeans, the pieds-noirsPYAY NWARA French term for the people of European birth or descent who lived in Algeria. It means black feet. See the glossary box., lived there among about nine million Muslim Arabs and Berbers. The Europeans held the best land and city jobs, and most of the political power. After the war, in May 1945, the day of victory in Europe, Muslim marchers at Sétif demanded freedom. Riots followed in which about a hundred Europeans were killed, and French reprisals killed thousands of Muslims, by some counts many more. Algerians did not forget it.

On November 1, 1954, a group calling itself the National Liberation Front, or FLN, set off bombs and attacked police posts across Algeria. The war it began was cruel on both sides. The FLN fought as guerrillasguh-RIL-uhA fighter who works in small bands, using ambush and surprise against a stronger army. See the glossary box. in the mountains and used terror in the cities. France sent more than four hundred thousand troops. Read more below. in 1957, French paratroopers broke the FLN’s network in the capital, but they used torture to do it, and the practice, once known in France, turned many French against the war. Muslims who served the French, the harkisHAR-keezAlgerian Muslims who served as soldiers or helpers in the French army during the Algerian war. See the glossary box., were caught in the middle.

In 1958 the army and the settlers threatened to overthrow the French government if it gave way, and the Fourth Republic fell. The general who was called back to power, Charles de GaulleSHARL duh GOHLCharles de Gaulle was the French general who led the Free French in the Second World War and later ended the war in Algeria. Read more below., first appeared to support the settlers. In time he decided that holding Algeria would cost France its future, and he offered Algerians a vote on their fate. In 1961 a group of generals tried to seize power, and failed. After eight years of war, the Evian agreements were signed in March 1962 and Algeria became independent on July 5. Almost all the pieds-noirs, about 800,000 people, left for France. Thousands of harkis who stayed were killed. Estimates of the Algerian dead range from three hundred thousand to over a million, and the number is still argued. The FLN built a one-party state.

🏯 CITY
Algiers

Algiers stands on a hillside above the Mediterranean. It had been a pirate port before the French took it in 1830, and under French rule it grew into a modern city with wide boulevards, a university, and a European quarter beside the old Muslim Casbah, a maze of narrow streets and steps.

In 1956 and 1957 the Casbah was the base of the FLN’s urban fighters, who planted bombs in cafes and shops. The French army then took control of the city and rooted them out, house by house. The film The Battle of Algiers, made in 1966, is still studied by soldiers and students. Algiers is now the capital of independent Algeria.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Charles de Gaulle
SHARL duh GOHL

Charles de Gaulle (1890 to 1970) was a career army officer who refused to accept the French defeat of 1940. He fled to London and led the Free French from exile. After the liberation he headed the first government and then withdrew, disgusted with party politics, in 1946.

In 1958 he returned, wrote a new constitution that gave France a strong president, and became its first president of the Fifth Republic. He gave Algeria its independence against the wishes of many of his supporters, and he granted independence to most of France’s African colonies as well. He resigned in 1969 and died the next year.

THE WIDER WAVE, AND THE QUESTION

Elsewhere, empires ended with less bloodshed. Ghana in 1957 was the first sub-Saharan colony to win its freedom from Britain, and in 1960 seventeen African nations became independent. Britain’s prime minister said in Cape Town that a wind of change was blowing through the continent. Others went hard. The Belgian Congo collapsed into civil war within days of its independence. Everywhere, new nations faced the old problem of making stable offices, common laws, and a people loyal to one another.

Now look at your two-column list. On the gift side are the parliaments, courts, schools, and languages that left behind a framework which several new nations used well. On the weapon side are the leaders who took the empires’ own words and held them to the standard: Gandhi quoting liberty to Britain, and the FLN citing the rights that France said it had given to all. Ho Chi Minh will make the same move in one of this week’s sources. Ask what a ruler gives up when he says that all men are born free, and ask also how well the new states themselves kept the promise.
