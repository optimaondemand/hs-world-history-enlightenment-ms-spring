TODAY'S FOCUS

In August 1914 the German army marched west with a plan to defeat France in about six weeks. Five weeks later, within sight of Paris, the plan fell apart. This lesson follows the plan from its birth to its failure, and the men who had to decide, with little time and poor news, while hundreds of thousands of lives hung on their choices. It asks one hard question. Was the plan doomed from the start, or did the choices of a few men decide the outcome? Keep that question in mind as you read. It is the subject of this week’s discussion.

A WAR PLAN BUILT ON SPEED

Germany had a problem of geography. France lay on its western border and Russia on its eastern one. In the 1890s those two nations signed a military alliance. If war came, Germany would have to fight both.

Instead, the German right wing would march through Belgium, curve west of Paris, and trap the French army against its own frontier.

Look at what this plan required. It required crossing a country that had promised to stay out of wars. THE PLAN CHANGES HANDS

Schlieffen retired in 1906. His successor was Helmuth von Moltke, called Moltke the Younger because his famous uncle had led Prussia’s armies to victory in the 1860s and 1870s. He strengthened the German left wing, which faced France’s border in Alsace and Lorraine. He also kept the army out of the Netherlands, so the right wing would have to squeeze through Belgium alone. Later, many officers argued that these changes left the right wing too weak. Others replied that Moltke had little choice, since the French might attack in the south.

In the summer of 1914 the plan met its test. On June 28 a Serbian nationalist shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austrian throne. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia a month later. Germany declared war on Russia that day and on France on August 3. On August 4 German troops crossed into Belgium. Britain declared war that night.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Helmuth von Moltke the Younger
HEL-moot fon MOLT-kuh

Helmuth von Moltke (1848 to 1916) became chief of the German General Staff in 1906. He was the nephew of the older Moltke, who had planned Prussia’s victories over Austria and France. Historians describe the younger Moltke as anxious, and he doubted his own fitness for the post.

He inherited Schlieffen’s plan and changed it. He kept the sweep through Belgium but strengthened the left wing and chose not to march through the Netherlands. In August 1914 he commanded from Luxembourg, far from the fighting, and depended on slow messages from his generals.

After the Marne he was replaced by Erich von Falkenhayn on September 14, though the change was kept quiet at first. Moltke died in 1916. His story shows how much a plan can depend on the nerve of one man.

AUGUST: THE PLAN MEETS REALITY

Germany had demanded free passage through Belgium, and Belgium had refused. Its small army fought harder than Berlin expected. The forts around the city of Liege held out for about ten days, until giant German siege guns pounded them to rubble. Ten days was a long time on a tight timetable. Brussels fell on August 20, but the German columns were already behind schedule.

France, meanwhile, attacked. Its own war plan sent armies charging into Alsace and Lorraine, lands Germany had taken in 1871. French soldiers advanced in blue coats and red trousers into the fire of German machine guns. On August 22 alone, about 27,000 French soldiers were killed. It was the worst single day in the history of the French army. The attack failed.

Yet the French army was not trapped. It could fall back, and it did. The British Expeditionary Force, a small professional army, fought its first battle at Mons on August 23 and then retreated south with the French. By the first days of September the Germans were about thirty miles from Paris, and the French government had left the capital.

But the Germans were worn out. Their soldiers had marched hundreds of miles in summer heat. Food and ammunition came forward slowly on horse-drawn wagons. Moltke directed the campaign from far behind the front, and news reached him late. Two army corps had also been taken from the right wing and sent east after Russia invaded East Prussia. They arrived too late to share in the German victory there.

THE MARNE

On September 2, General Alexander von Kluck, who commanded the First Army on the far right of the German line, made a choice. His orders said to swing west of Paris. He used France’s railways to move whole units behind the front, and he fired generals who failed him. On September 5 the new army struck Kluck’s flank. Kluck pulled units back to meet it. A gap about thirty miles wide opened between his army and the German Second Army. British and French troops moved into the gap.

Moltke could not see the battle. On September 8 he sent a staff officer, Lieutenant Colonel Richard Hentsch, to visit the armies and judge the danger. Hentsch found the Second Army ready to retreat and agreed. On September 9 the First Army fell back too. By September 12 the Germans were digging in north of the Aisne River. Two days later Moltke was replaced. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Joseph Joffre
zhoh-FRUH

Joseph Joffre (1852 to 1931) was a French general known for his calm. He was trained as an engineer, not as a staff officer, and became commander in chief of the French army in 1911. His soldiers called him “Papa Joffre.” He ate regular meals, slept well, and kept his temper, even in the worst days of August 1914.

His war plan sent French armies into Alsace and Lorraine, and it failed with terrible losses. Joffre did not panic. He fired generals who failed, moved troops across France by rail, and chose the Marne as the place to turn and fight. For this he was hailed as the savior of France.

When the long battles of 1916 went badly, he lost his command. He was made a Marshal of France in December of that year.

🛡 BATTLE
The Battle of the Marne

The Battle of the Marne was fought from September 5 to 12, 1914, along the Marne River east of Paris. French and British forces counterattacked a German army that had come within thirty miles of the capital. The gap between the German First and Second Armies let Allied troops push forward, and the Germans retreated.

A famous story tells how about 600 Paris taxis carried some 6,000 soldiers to the front. The number was small, but the taxis became a symbol of a nation fighting for its life.

The battle ended Germany’s hope of a quick victory in the west. Each side lost hundreds of thousands of men killed, wounded, or missing. After the Marne, the war in the west became a long struggle.

WHAT DECIDED IT?

Historians still argue about the Marne. The argument matters because it asks how much a war depends on plans and how much on people.

One view says the plan was sound and men broke it. Moltke weakened the right wing. Kluck turned the wrong way. Hentsch ordered a retreat while the fighting was still undecided, and the First Army obeyed. On this view, steadier nerves might have meant a German victory in 1914.

The other view says the plan could not have worked. Some military historians point to supply. No army of that size could be fed and armed so far from its railways, whoever led it. Others point to Belgium. The invasion brought Britain into the war and turned world opinion against Germany. A plan that needed Britain to stay out was built on a hope, not a fact.

Notice also what the Marne shows about character. Joffre was calm and held his nerve when his own plan had failed. Moltke was a worried man who lost his grip. The old writers called the first quality self-government: the mind ruling fear instead of being ruled by it. Great plans do not carry themselves out. They depend on the courage and judgment of the men who command. Belgium offers a second example. A small country chose to resist a great one, and the ten days it cost the German timetable were part of the answer to this lesson’s question.

After the Marne, both sides tried to outflank each other in a race to the sea. By November a line of trenches ran from the Swiss border to the North Sea. The short war was over. The next lesson follows the soldiers into the trenches.
