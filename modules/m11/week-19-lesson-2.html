TODAY'S FOCUS

After the Marne, both sides expected to move again. They did not. For more than three years the Western Front stayed almost where it was. This lesson takes you into the trenches and through three great battles: Ypres, Verdun, and the Somme. Each tried to break the deadlock, and each failed in its own way. As you read, ask what kind of men, and what kind of nations, could keep fighting through such losses.

THE RACE TO THE SEA AND YPRES

After the Marne, each army tried to swing around the other’s open flankFLANGKThe side of an army. It is the weakest part, because soldiers face forward. See the glossary box. to the north. The result was a race to the sea. It ended in October and November 1914 around the Belgian town of Ypres. (British soldiers called it “Wipers.”) In the First Battle of Ypres the Germans attacked again and again, and British, French, and Belgian troops held. The small British professional army, the men who had marched to Mons, was nearly destroyed. Germany lost many young volunteers too, including students who had enlisted in the first excitement of the war.

By winter a line of trenches ran about four hundred miles from the Swiss border to the North Sea. Neither side could get around it.

LIFE IN THE TRENCHES

Why did the armies dig in? The answer is firepower. Machine guns and rapid-fire rifles could cut down an advancing line in seconds. Artillery was even deadlier, and shell fragments caused most of the wounds of the war. A soldier who stood up was likely to be hit. A soldier who dug in could survive.

So each side built a maze of trenches. A front line faced the enemy. Behind it ran support trenches and reserve trenches, joined by narrow passages. In front lay barbed wire and then no man's landNOH manz LANDThe open ground between two armies’ trenches. See the glossary box., the shell-torn ground between the armies. In places it was only a few hundred feet wide. Trenches did not run in straight lines. They zigzagged, so that one shell or one enemy rifleman could not sweep a long stretch. Soldiers spent days in the front line and then rotated to the rear. At night, patrols crept into no man’s land to repair wire, listen to the enemy, or raid his trenches. They lived with mud, rats, and lice. In wet weather their feet could rot, a condition called trench foot. French soldiers called themselves poiluspwah-LOOFrench slang for an infantry soldier. 🔧 TECHNOLOGY
Poison Gas

The Germans used chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres on April 22, 1915. The gas drifted over the French lines, and soldiers who breathed it choked as it burned their lungs. Later in the war, armies added deadlier gases, including phosgene and mustard gas, and fired them in shells as well as releasing them from cylinders.

Gas killed far fewer men than artillery did. But it spread terror, and it injured many soldiers for life. It forced every army to develop gas masks, and soldiers had to learn to put them on within seconds. A gas alarm could turn a quiet night into a scramble in the dark.

After the war, many nations agreed to ban the use of poison gas in war, though the weapons were never fully forgotten.

ATTRITION

By 1915 the generals faced a puzzle. Frontal attacks cost huge numbers of men and gained little ground. Some commanders came to believe in attritionuh-TRISH-unWearing down an enemy, bit by bit, until he can no longer fight. See the glossary box.: wearing the enemy down until he could no longer fight. If each side lost men at the same rate, the side with more men and more factories would win in the end.

This was a cold way to think about war. It also helped explain why the leaders of every country kept going. None could stop without admitting defeat to their own people. The war had become a test of which nation could bear the most.

VERDUN, 1916

In February 1916 the German commander, Erich von Falkenhayn, attacked the French fortress city of Verdun. He later claimed that he wanted to “bleed France white.” Verdun mattered to French pride, so he expected France to defend it at any cost. The attack began on February 21 with a huge bombardmentbom-BARD-muntA long, heavy attack with artillery. See the glossary box.. German troops soon captured Fort Douaumont, a key fort that was almost undefended.

France held on. General Philippe Pétainfee-LEEP pay-TANPhilippe Pétain was the French general who organized the defense of Verdun and became a national hero. Read more below. took command and organized the defense. A single road, later called the Sacred Way, carried trucks day and night with men and shells. Pétain rotated divisions in and out, so that most of the French army served at Verdun. The battle lasted until December. Roughly 700,000 men on both sides were killed, wounded, or missing. The French line held, and Verdun became a symbol of France’s will to endure. But the cost to both sides was terrible, and the front hardly moved.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Philippe Pétain
fee-LEEP pay-TAN

Philippe Pétain (1856 to 1951) was a French general close to retirement when the war began. He was known as a careful commander who cared about his men and distrusted wasteful attacks. He rose quickly after 1914, and in February 1916 he took command at Verdun.

He organized the supply road, rotated tired divisions out, and relied on artillery to protect his infantry. France hailed him as the hero of Verdun. In 1917, after mutinies broke out in the French army, he restored order by improving conditions, granting leave, and ending hopeless offensives. He became a Marshal of France in 1918.

In 1940 he would lead the French government that made peace with Germany. You will meet that part of his story in Quarter 4.

THE SOMME, 1916

Britain was now fighting with a huge new army of volunteers. In the summer of 1916 its commander, Sir Douglas Haig, planned a great offensive along the Somme River. It was meant to relieve Verdun and break the German line. A week of artillery fire came first. Haig hoped it would destroy the German barbed wire and dugouts. But many shells did not explode, and German soldiers waited in deep shelters.

On July 1 the British infantry climbed out of their trenches and walked toward the German lines. Machine guns met them. The British army suffered about 57,000 casualties that day, nearly 20,000 of them killed. It remains the bloodiest day in the history of the British army. Whole battalions of friends who had enlisted together, called “Pals” battalions, were nearly wiped out in minutes.

The battle went on until November. British, French, and German losses together passed one million men. The Allies gained only about six miles. In September the British used the first tanks, but there were too few, and they broke down too often to change the result.

WHAT KEPT THEM GOING?

Why did the armies not break, and why did the nations not quit? This war was fought largely by citizens, not only by professionals. Men had enlisted out of love of country and loyalty to friends, and courage in the trenches was real and often silent. But courage alone does not explain it. Governments controlled the news, and leaders feared the political price of stopping.

The philosopher Hegel called war the test of a state’s health. By 1917 that test would break some states and harden others. The next lessons ask which nations held together under the strain, and why.
