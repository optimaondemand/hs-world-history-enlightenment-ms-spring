TODAY'S FOCUS

In the autumn of 1939 the war looked like a repeat of 1914. In the spring of 1940 it became something no one had foreseen. Within six weeks Germany broke the army that had held it for four years in the last war, and the British army escaped across the sea with little more than its rifles. This lesson follows Poland, Norway, France, and the beach at Dunkirk. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: was Dunkirk a victory or a defeat?

THE QUIET WINTER

On the Western Front almost nothing happened. British soldiers called it the Phoney War. The French army sat behind the Maginot Line, a chain of forts along the German border, and a British expeditionary forceek-spuh-DISH-uh-nair-ee FORSAn army sent to fight in a foreign land. See the glossary box. took its place beside the French in the north. The Allied plan was to wait, build up, and win a long war by cutting off German trade, as they had in 1918.

Elsewhere the war went on. In November 1939 Stalin attacked Finland and found the small Finnish army hard to beat. The Finns held out for three months before they gave up land. In April 1940 Germany struck Denmark and Norway to protect its supply of Swedish iron ore and to gain naval bases. Denmark gave in within hours. Norway fought for two months, and British help to Norway failed.

The failure caused a political storm in London. On May 8 many members of Neville Chamberlain’s own party voted against him or stayed away. On May 10 he resigned. The King asked Chamberlain who should follow him. Chamberlain wanted Lord Halifax, the foreign secretary, but Halifax declined, saying a peer in the House of Lords would find it hard to lead the war. So the post went to Winston Churchill. Notice how narrow the choice was. A different answer from one man on one afternoon would have given Britain a different leader in its darkest hour.

THE SICKLE CUT

On May 10, the same day Churchill took office, Germany attacked the Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg. The Allies had expected this, and they had a plan. The best French and British armies moved north into Belgium to meet the Germans along a river line. That was what the German plan wanted them to do. The plan came from General Erich von Manstein, and its name was SichelschnittZIKH-el-shnitA German word for sickle cut. It names the plan to drive tanks through the Ardennes to the Channel and trap the Allied armies. See the glossary box.. The main German thrust would not go through Belgium at all. It would pass through the Ardennes, a hilly forest that French generals thought too rough for tanks, and then race to the English Channel to cut off the armies that had gone north. Hitler liked the plan when he heard it, and it replaced a more cautious one.

It worked. Seven of Germany’s ten panzer divisions took the Ardennes road. On May 13 Heinz GuderianHYNTS goo-DAIR-ee-unHeinz Guderian was the German general whose tank corps broke the French line at Sedan in May 1940. Read more below.’s corps crossed the Meuse River at Sedan, after dive bombers pinned down the French defenders. The line there was held by second-rate reserve troops, and many of them ran. By May 20 German tanks had reached the Channel coast near Abbeville. The best Allied armies, hundreds of thousands of men, were cut off in the north.

Notice what had not decided the matter. The Allies had about as many tanks as the Germans, and many of theirs were better built. The French had spread theirs among the infantry. The Germans massed theirs, gave them radios, and backed them with aircraft. The French commander, General Maurice Gamelin, was replaced by General Maxime Weygand on May 19, but by then little time was left.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Heinz Guderian
HYNTS goo-DAIR-ee-un

Heinz Guderian (1888 to 1954) was a career army officer who studied the tank closely in the years after the Great War. He believed that tanks should work together in large groups, with radios, instead of serving as slow support for foot soldiers. In 1937 he wrote a book that laid out these ideas.

In May 1940 he commanded the corps that crossed the Meuse and drove to the Channel. Some of his superiors feared he was moving too fast, and at one point they ordered him to stop. He argued with them and kept going. Later he led tanks in Russia, where Hitler dismissed him in 1941 and again recalled him in 1943. He ended the war as chief of the army staff, and he wrote a famous memoir.

THE HALT AT DUNKIRK

The trapped armies fell back to the port of Dunkirk, the last harbor they held. On May 24 the German tanks, now close to the town, stopped for about two days. Hitler approved the halt on the advice of General Gerd von Rundstedt. Historians still argue about why. The tanks needed rest and repair. Hermann Göring boasted that his Luftwaffe could finish the job. Hitler may also have wanted to save his armor for the battle for the rest of France. Whatever the reasons, the pause gave the British time to strengthen the ring around the port.

On May 26 the Royal Navy began Operation DynamoDunkirkDunkirk was the French port from which the British and French armies were taken off by sea between May 26 and June 4, 1940. Read more below., the plan to take the army home. Destroyers and ferries loaded men from the harbor wall. Hundreds of small boats, from fishing boats to pleasure yachts, crossed the Channel to take men off the beaches. In nine days about 338,000 British, French, and Belgian soldiers reached England. French soldiers held the edge of the ring to the end, and about 40,000 of them were captured when it closed. The British army left behind nearly all its tanks, guns, and trucks.

The country welcomed the soldiers as if they were winners. Churchill refused to join in. On June 4 he told Parliament that “wars are not won by evacuations.” You will read more of that speech this week.

🛡 BATTLE
Dunkirk

Dunkirk is a port on the northern coast of France, a short sail from the southeast tip of England. Its beaches are wide and shallow, so large ships could not come close to the shore. The harbor had a long wooden pier, called the East Mole, and men walked along it to board the ships.

German aircraft bombed the port day after day, and the town was in flames. The RAF fought them off over the Channel, and many pilots flew several times a day. The beach itself was crowded with men who waited in long lines in the sand and the surf. About 200 ships were lost during the operation, but the numbers taken off grew each day, from about 8,000 on the first full day to over 68,000 on the busiest.

THE FALL OF FRANCE

Weygand tried to hold a new line along the Somme and the Aisne rivers. The Germans struck on June 5 and broke it within days. Italy joined the war on June 10, hoping to share the spoils. The French government fled from Paris, and German troops entered the undefended city on June 14. Premier Paul Reynaud wanted to fight on from North Africa. He was outvoted and resigned. The armistice was signed on June 22 at Compiègne, in the same railway car where Germany had signed in 1918. Hitler had it brought out of its museum for the day. Germany took the north and the Atlantic coast. The rest of France was governed from the spa town of Vichy by Pétain’s government, which kept its own army and navy but depended on German goodwill. On June 18 a little-known French general named Charles de Gaulle spoke on the radio from London. He told the French that France had lost a battle but not the war.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Philippe Pétain
fee-LEEP pay-TAN

Philippe Pétain (1856 to 1951) became a national hero in 1916 when he defended Verdun, and he was made a marshal of France in 1918. He was known as a careful general who cared about the lives of his soldiers. By 1940 he was eighty-four years old.

When Reynaud resigned he became head of the government, and he argued that France must stop fighting. Many French people saw him as a father figure who had saved them from further ruin. His Vichy government ended the republic, with its motto of liberty, equality, and fraternity, and replaced it with a state built on work, family, and country. It also passed laws against Jews and helped the Germans. After the war he was tried for treason and sentenced to death, but the sentence was changed to life in prison. He died on an island prison in 1951.
