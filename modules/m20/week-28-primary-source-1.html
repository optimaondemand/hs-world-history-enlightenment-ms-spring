PRIMARY SOURCE 1
Directive No. 21, Operation Barbarossa (December 18, 1940)
Hitler orders his armed forces to prepare to destroy the Soviet Union.
💡 BEFORE YOU READ

A directive was a written order from Hitler as supreme commander to the heads of the German armed forces. Directive No. 21 went out in December 1940, six months before the attack. Only a few copies were made, and each was numbered. The German army’s own plans had been under study since the summer, and the directive turned them into a firm order.

After the war the directive was read aloud as evidence at the Nuremberg trial of the main Nazi leaders. A few terms may be new. “Reservation” means a condition attached to an order. “Camouflaging” means hiding. “Armored wedges” are tank forces that drive deep into enemy ground like the point of a wedge.

Read this as a historian should. Ask who is giving the order and who must obey it. Ask what Hitler wants done, and what he says about timing and secrecy. Ask what the order assumes about the Soviet army and about Britain.

The German Armed Forces must be prepared to crush Soviet Russia in a quick campaign (Case Barbarossa) even before the conclusion of the war against England.

For this purpose the Army will have to employ all available units, with the reservation that the occupied territories must be secured against surprises.

[...]

The mass of the Russian Army in Western Russia is to be destroyed in daring operations by driving forward deep armored wedges, and the retreat of units capable of combat into the vast Russian interior is to be prevented.

[...]

The ultimate objective of the operation is to erect a barrier against Asiatic Russia on the general line Volga-Archangel.

[...]

The preparations requiring more time to start are to be begun now, if this has not yet been done, and are to be completed by May 15, 1941.

Great caution has to be exercised that the intention of an attack will not be recognized.

📌 A NOTE FOR YOU

Notice the phrase “even before the conclusion of the war against England.” Ask what it tells you about Hitler’s view of Britain’s chances. Notice also how much of the order is about speed and surprise, and compare it with the plan you met in Lesson 1. You can return to this page as often as you like.
