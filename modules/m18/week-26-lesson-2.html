TODAY'S FOCUS

At the same time that China was torn apart, the island empire of Japan was changing. In the 1920s it had a lively parliament and a growing economy. By the late 1930s its army ruled the country and was fighting a huge war in China. This lesson asks how that happened and who was responsible. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: was Japan’s road to war chosen or forced?

A RISING POWER

In 1868 a group of young samurai overthrew the shogun, the military ruler, and restored the emperor to power. The new government, under the Emperor Meiji, set out to make Japan strong. It built railways, factories, a modern army and navy, and a school system. Japan defeated China in 1895 and then, to the shock of the world, defeated Russia in 1905. It took Korea in 1910. It fought on the Allied side in the First World War and sat at the Paris conference as one of the great powers.

In the 1920s Japan had political parties, a parliament, and in 1925 the vote for all adult men. Japan also signed treaties that limited its navy. But the system had a weakness. By law, the army and navy ministers had to be serving officers. If the military disliked a cabinet, its minister could resign, and the cabinet would fall. Civil leaders could never fully control the army. This fact would matter a great deal. Japan also had very few natural resources. It had to buy oil, iron, and rubber from abroad, and it feared being cut off.

DEPRESSION AND DISCONTENT

The world Depression hit Japan hard. Silk, its biggest export to the United States, collapsed in price. In the northern villages, farmers went hungry, and some sold their daughters. Meanwhile many young officers, who came from poor country families, grew angry at party politicians and at rich business families, whom they thought corrupt and selfish. They taught that the nation needed a stronger hand, and that the empire’s future lay on the Asian mainland. MUKDEN

Japan held special rights in Manchuria, the rich northeastern region of China, including the South Manchuria Railway. A Japanese army, the Kwantung Army, guarded it. On the night of September 18, 1931, a small bomb exploded on the railway track near the city of Mukden. The damage was so slight that a train passed over the spot soon after. But the Kwantung Army claimed that Chinese soldiers had done it, and it attacked. Within a few months it had overrun all of Manchuria. We now know that Japanese officers had set the bomb themselves.

The government in Tokyo told the army to stop. The army did not obey, and the government did not dare punish it. The public cheered the victories. The League of Nations sent a commission under the English Lord Lytton to investigate. Its report said that the Japanese attack was not self-defense and that Manchukuo had not come about by the free choice of its people. When the League voted in February 1933 to accept that report, Japan walked out and later left the League. The League had no way to enforce its verdict, and the world’s great powers did nothing. 👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Emperor Hirohito
hee-roh-HEE-toh

Hirohito (1901 to 1989) became emperor in 1926 and took the reign name Showa. Under the constitution he was sacred, but in daily affairs he acted on the advice of his ministers. He studied marine biology and had traveled in Europe as a young man.

Historians disagree about how much he could have done. He did step in forcefully in February 1936, when he ordered rebel officers to be put down. In 1945 he ordered Japan to surrender. But most of the time he accepted the army’s plans, and he did not stop the war in China. He died in 1989 after the longest reign in Japanese history.

THE ROAD TO 1937

In February 1936 more than a thousand young soldiers revolted in Tokyo and murdered several leaders, including a former prime minister. Emperor Hirohito ordered the revolt put down, and it ended in a few days. But the army’s leaders used the scare to gain more power in the cabinet. By then Japan had also signed a pact with Nazi Germany against communism.

In July 1937, at the Marco Polo Bridge near Beijing, Japanese and Chinese soldiers exchanged shots after a night exercise. It may have been an accident, but neither side backed down. Japan sent in more troops, and Chiang Kai-shek decided that China would fight. Both sides joined in a bitter struggle at Shanghai in August, where the Chinese army lost its best troops. The Nationalist government fell back up the Yangzi River, and in December the Japanese army reached the capital, Nanjing. Scholars still debate the total of the dead. The Tokyo war crimes court counted more than two hundred thousand, and the Chinese government says three hundred thousand, while some Japanese historians give lower figures of tens of thousands. All agree that a terrible slaughter took place.

A few foreigners worked to save lives. A German businessman and Nazi Party member named John Rabe led an international committee that set up a Safety Zone, where about two hundred thousand Chinese found shelter. American missionaries and doctors helped. Their diaries and letters are among our best evidence of what happened. The Chinese government moved inland to Chongqing, and the war went on for eight more years. 🏯 CITY
Nanjing

Nanjing, on the Yangzi River, was the capital of several Chinese dynasties. Its name means ‘southern capital.’ The first Ming emperor built a great wall around it, and the mausoleum of Sun Yat-sen stands on a hill outside.

Chiang Kai-shek made it the capital of Nationalist China in 1927, and its government built wide avenues, new offices, and schools. In 1937 it held about a million people. After the Chinese army withdrew, the Japanese entered on December 13. In the weeks that followed, tens of thousands, and by some counts hundreds of thousands, were killed. The city is remembered today as the site of one of the worst crimes of the war.

CHOSEN OR FORCED?

Some historians say Japan was pushed. The Depression cut its exports. The United States had banned Japanese immigration in 1924, and high tariffs closed foreign markets. A weak and divided China offered land and raw materials, and the Soviet Union was a threat to the north. On this view a poor island nation faced a hard choice.

Others stress the choices Japan’s leaders made. The same Japan had signed naval treaties in 1930 and kept a parliament in the 1920s. At each step in 1931, 1936, and 1937 there was a way to stop, and the civil government and the Emperor did not take it. The army made policy by violence, and was rewarded each time. A state in which armed officers answer to no civil authority has failed at the first task of government, to keep the use of force under law. Notice that the choice was made by a few men, often junior officers, while many Japanese did not agree. Next week’s lessons turn to Europe again, where the road to war was being laid by Hitler.
