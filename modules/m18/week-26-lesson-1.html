TODAY'S FOCUS

While Europe drifted toward dictatorship, the largest country in Asia was breaking apart. China had no strong government, and two rival parties were fighting for its future. One party had an army, a capital, and foreign friends. The other had almost nothing, and it survived a retreat of six thousand miles. This lesson follows that struggle and the men who led it. It sets the scene for next lesson, when Japan struck.

AN EMPIRE FALLS

China’s last dynasty, the Qing, had ruled since 1644. In the nineteenth century it was battered by foreign powers. Britain, France, Russia, Japan, and others forced it to open trading ports and to give them special rights in those ports. In 1911 a revolt broke out, and in February 1912 the child emperor, Puyi, gave up the throne. The revolution’s best-known leader, Sun Yat-sen, wanted a republic built on what he called the Three Principles of the People: national independence, democratic rights, and a decent livelihood for all. He was not strong enough to hold power.

A general named Yuan Shikai took over and tried to make himself emperor. When he died in 1916, the country broke up. For the next twelve years China was ruled by warlordWOR-lordA military leader who rules a region with a private army, and who is not under the control of a central government. See the glossary box.s, generals who held provinces with their own private armies and taxed the people as they pleased. The government in Beijing had no real power. Farmers were robbed, soldiers fought one another, and foreigners kept their special rights.

In 1919 the Paris peace conference gave Germany’s old rights in the Chinese province of Shandong to Japan, although China had fought on the Allied side. On May 4, thousands of students marched in Beijing to protest. This May Fourth Movement was a turning point. It stirred a strong new national feeling and made many young Chinese look for new answers, from the West and from Russia.

In 1921 a small group of intellectuals founded the Chinese Communist Party in Shanghai. At first it had only a few dozen members. The Nationalists opened a military academy at Whampoa, near Guangzhou, and put a young officer named Chiang Kai-shekjee-AHNG ky-SHEKChiang Kai-shek was the Nationalist general who unified China under the Kuomintang in 1928 and led it against both the Communists and Japan. Read more below. in charge. Sun died in 1925, and Chiang became the party’s leader. In 1926 he led an army north against the warlords in a campaign called the Northern Expedition. It was a great success. Yet Chiang distrusted his Communist allies, and on April 12, 1927, in Shanghai, his troops and allied gangsters turned on them. Thousands of Communists and labor leaders were killed in the following weeks. The united front was over.

By 1928 the Nationalists held Nanjing as their capital, and most warlords had accepted Chiang’s rule, at least in name. China seemed to have a government at last. But Chiang’s power rested on generals who were loyal to him personally, and on landlords and bankers in the cities. The countryside, where nine in ten Chinese lived, was left much as before.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Chiang Kai-shek
jee-AHNG ky-SHEK

Chiang Kai-shek (1887 to 1975) was born in Zhejiang province. He trained as a soldier in Japan, took part in the 1911 revolution, and became Sun Yat-sen’s trusted officer. After Sun’s death he led the Nationalists, defeated the warlords, and made Nanjing the capital in 1927.

Chiang was disciplined, stubborn, and a believer in order. He married Soong Mei-ling in 1927 and later became a Christian. He was suspicious of rivals, including those in his own party, and his government often failed to reach the poor. After the Communist victory in 1949, he led the Nationalists to the island of Taiwan, where he governed until he died.

MAO AND THE VILLAGE

The Communists who survived the killings of 1927 fled to the mountains and villages. One of them was Mao ZedongMOW ZUH-dahngMao Zedong was the Communist leader who built a peasant army, survived the Long March, and later ruled China as head of the Communist Party. Read more below., the son of a Hunan farmer. In a report written that spring Mao argued that China’s peasants, not its few city workers, could make a revolution. Soviet advisers had taught the opposite. In the hills of Jiangxi province Mao and the soldier Zhu De built a Red Army and a small Communist state, the Jiangxi Soviet.

Mao’s army fought as guerrillaguh-RIL-uhA fighter in a small, unofficial band that attacks by surprise instead of fighting in large open battles. See the glossary box.s. The word is Spanish, and it means “little war.” Small bands avoided big battles with stronger enemies. They struck by surprise, then melted away into the countryside. The Communists also won friends among poor peasants by seizing the land of landlords and sharing it out. Chiang called them bandits and launched five great “encirclement campaigns” against them. The fifth, in 1933 and 1934, used a German adviser’s plan. Chiang’s armies built lines of small forts and cut off the Communists’ food. The Jiangxi Soviet began to starve.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Mao Zedong
MOW ZUH-dahng

Mao Zedong (1893 to 1976) was born into a farming family in Hunan province. He worked in a university library in Beijing, read widely, and was one of the founders of the Chinese Communist Party in 1921. After 1927 he came to believe that China’s revolution would be won in the countryside.

Mao was a capable organizer, a vivid writer, and a hard and patient man. He gained leadership of the party during the Long March. In 1949 he proclaimed the People’s Republic of China. His later campaigns, such as the Great Leap Forward, brought famine and death to tens of millions. He died in 1976.

THE LONG MARCH

In October 1934 about eighty-six thousand Communist soldiers and officials broke out of the Nationalist ring and began a long retreat. Their route curved west and then north through some of the harshest country in China. They crossed mountain ranges, swift rivers, and a high grassland of swamp where many died of cold and hunger. In May 1935 they won the crossing of the Dadu River by seizing an iron chain bridge at Luding. They were bombed from the air and followed by Nationalist troops all the way.

In January 1935, at a meeting in Zunyi, the Communists blamed their defeats on their leaders and chose Mao to take charge. After more than a year, and about six thousand miles, the marchers reached the dry hills of Shaanxi in the north in October 1935. Perhaps ten thousand were left, though other columns joined them later. The march is a legend in China. It showed that the party could endure a terrible hardship, and it made Mao’s name. Notice, though, that in 1935 the party looked nearly dead.

THE KIDNAPPING AT XI'AN

Japan had already seized the northeast of China, and Chiang’s policy was to crush the Communists first and fight the Japanese later. Many Chinese, including some of his own generals, disagreed. In December 1936 Chiang flew to the city of Xi’an to push his generals to attack the Communists again. One of them, Zhang Xueliang, whose father had been a Manchurian warlord and whose homeland Japan had taken, had Chiang arrested.

Some Communists wanted Chiang tried and shot. Moscow, which wanted a strong China to resist Japan, urged his release. Chiang came out alive after two weeks, and agreed to a second united front. When Japan attacked in 1937, as you will see next lesson, the Nationalists and the Communists joined, though they never ceased to distrust one another. Zhang Xueliang was held under guard for most of the rest of his long life.

WHAT HISTORY DID NOT FORCE

Nothing here was certain. If Chiang’s fifth campaign had trapped the marchers at the river, or if his fort lines had been built a little faster, the Chinese Communists might have been destroyed. If Zhang Xueliang had not acted, Chiang might have kept up his war on the Communists, and Japan might have faced a divided China. Mao rose by a mix of skill and good fortune.

Chiang did well at the first task of a state, to make a lasting national government, but he failed at others. His government was corrupt, and it did little for the peasants. Mao’s party took on the second hard task, to give the poor a stake in the new order, but it paid for it with violence. Both lessons will return when the war comes.
