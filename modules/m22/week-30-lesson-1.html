TODAY'S FOCUS

By the end of 1941 Germany was bogged down in Russia, and the United States had joined the war. In the next three years the tide of the war turned. This lesson follows the great turning points, from the Volga River to the sands of Egypt, the beaches of Normandy, and the forests of the Ardennes. It also prepares you for the lesson that follows, which deals with a crime that these armies were still working to stop.

STALINGRAD

In the summer of 1942 Hitler aimed his new offensive at the oil of the Caucasus. Part of his army was sent toward the city of Stalingrad, on the Volga River. It bore the name of the Soviet dictator, and it was a center of industry. General Friedrich Paulus’s Sixth Army fought its way into the ruins in September, and for two months the two sides fought from house to house. The Soviet defenders under General Vasily Chuikov held a narrow strip along the river bank, and they would not give it up.

The Soviet high command, led by Georgy ZhukovGAY-or-gee ZHOO-kawfGeorgy Zhukov was the Soviet marshal who planned the defense of Moscow and the encirclement at Stalingrad. Read more below., was preparing a trap. The German flanks north and south of the city were held by Romanian armies, which were poorly armed. On November 19 the Soviets struck both flanks. In four days the two arms met, and about 250,000 German and allied soldiers were caught in an encirclementen-SUR-kul-mentSurrounding an enemy force on every side so that it cannot escape or receive supplies. See the glossary box.. Hermann Göring promised to supply them by air, but the planes could not carry enough. Paulus asked to break out, and Hitler refused. Paulus surrendered on February 2, 1943, a day after Hitler promoted him to field marshal. About 91,000 men went into captivity, and only a few thousand ever came home.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Georgy Zhukov
GAY-or-gee ZHOO-kawf

Georgy Zhukov (1896 to 1974) was a peasant’s son who joined the cavalry in the Great War. He rose in the Red Army and won a victory over the Japanese in 1939. He survived Stalin’s purge of the officers when many of his comrades did not.

In the autumn of 1941 he organized the defense of Moscow. He then planned or directed many of the great Soviet offensives, including Stalingrad, and he led the troops that took Berlin in 1945. He was a hard and demanding commander who accepted heavy losses. After the war Stalin sent him away from Moscow, because he feared his fame. He came back to high office after Stalin died.

EL ALAMEIN AND THE MEDITERRANEAN

In North Africa General Erwin Rommel’s Afrika KorpsAH-free-kah KORA German phrase for Africa Corps. It names the German army force that fought in North Africa under Rommel. See the glossary box. came within sixty miles of Alexandria in the summer of 1942. If he took Egypt he could reach the Suez Canal and the oil of the Middle East. A British line held at El Alamein. In August General Bernard Montgomery took command of the British Eighth Army. On October 23 he opened his attack with a vast barrage of artillery, and after twelve days Rommel’s weakened army had to retreat. Churchill said of the victory that it was not the end, nor even the beginning of the end, but perhaps the end of the beginning.

On November 8, American and British troops landed in Morocco and Algeria in Operation Torch, a large amphibious assaultam-FIB-ee-us uh-SAWLTAn attack in which soldiers land from ships onto a hostile shore. See the glossary box. under General Dwight Eisenhower. The Axis forces in Tunisia held out until May 1943, when about 250,000 surrendered. The Allies then took Sicily in July, and Mussolini fell from power on July 25. Italy changed sides in September, but German troops seized the country and fought on. The war in Italy became a slow, costly climb up mountain valleys that lasted until 1945.

At Tehran in November 1943, Roosevelt, Churchill, and Stalin met for the first time and agreed that the main Anglo-American attack on France would come in the spring. Eisenhower, who had learned to lead a mixed army of Americans, British, and others in Africa and Italy, was named Supreme Commander. Meanwhile Allied bombers struck German factories and cities by day and night, and that bombing is still argued over, both for its results and for its cost in civilian lives.

The date was set for June 5, but a storm blew in. EisenhowerDWYT EYE-zun-how-erDwight Eisenhower was the American general who commanded the Allied invasion of France in 1944. Read more below. had to choose whether to wait two weeks or to go on the next day, during a short break in the weather that his forecasters had seen. He decided to go. The Germans thought no one would cross the Channel in such weather, and Rommel, who commanded the coastal defenses, had gone home for his wife’s birthday. Early on June 6 paratroopers dropped behind the beaches. At dawn about 156,000 troops came ashore from nearly 7,000 vessels at five beaches, Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno, and Sword.

At Omaha the Americans met the worst fire, and thousands were killed or wounded before small groups climbed the bluffs. By night all five beaches were in Allied hands. The German tanks held in reserve could not move without Hitler’s leave, and he was said to be asleep. After weeks of hard fighting in the hedgerows, an American breakout in late July trapped a German army at Falaise. Paris was freed on August 25. In the east, a vast Soviet offensive in June destroyed the German Army Group Center. Germany now faced a collapse on two sides.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Dwight Eisenhower
DWYT EYE-zun-how-er

Dwight Eisenhower (1890 to 1969) grew up in Kansas and graduated from West Point. He never led troops in battle in the Great War, but he proved to be a gifted planner. In 1942 General George Marshall chose him to command American forces in Europe.

His great skill was keeping a mixed team of proud and quarrelsome commanders working together. As Supreme Commander he led the invasion of France and the advance into Germany. On the eve of D-Day he wrote a short note taking full blame in case the landing failed, which you will read this week. He later served as president of the United States from 1953 to 1961.

🛡 BATTLE
Omaha Beach

Omaha was a curved beach about five miles long, backed by steep bluffs. The Germans had built strong points on the heights, with machine guns and mortars that covered every foot of sand. Underwater obstacles and mines lined the shore.

Strong currents and bad visibility pushed many landing craft off course, and the planes and ships that were to destroy the defenses missed most of their targets. Many of the tanks that were meant to support the infantry sank. The first wave struggled across open sand under fire. About two thousand Americans were killed or wounded there on the first day, but small groups of men took the heights one position at a time and held the beach.

THE BULGE

By the autumn of 1944 Allied armies stood at Germany’s western border. Hitler planned one last gamble. He would repeat the stroke of 1940 and attack through the Ardennes to take the port of Antwerp and split the Allies. He hoped that the alliance of Americans and British would break. On December 16, in fog that kept Allied planes on the ground, twenty-eight German divisions struck a thinly held American front.

The Germans drove a bulge, a salientSAY-lee-entA part of a battle line that sticks out into enemy ground and can be attacked from the sides. See the glossary box., deep into the American lines. At the crossroads town of Bastogne the 101st Airborne Division was surrounded. When the Germans demanded their surrender, the American commander, General Anthony McAuliffe, answered with one word: “Nuts!” The weather cleared, Allied planes flew again, and on December 26 General George Patton’s army broke through to Bastogne. By late January the bulge was gone. About nineteen thousand Americans were killed. Germany had spent its last reserves of men and fuel, and the way into Germany lay open.

Notice how often the course of a battle depended on a single decision, such as Hitler’s order to stand fast, or Eisenhower’s choice about the weather.
