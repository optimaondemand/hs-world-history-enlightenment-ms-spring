TODAY'S FOCUS

This lesson deals with the murder of the Jews of Europe. It is among the hardest parts of the history you will study, and it deserves to be read slowly and with respect. The facts matter more than any feeling about them, and we state them plainly. The lesson asks how persecution became murder, what the Wannsee Conference was, and what the Allied governments knew. It prepares you for this week’s discussion question: what did the Allies know, and what could they have done?

FROM PERSECUTION TO MURDER

You have already seen how the Nazis took away the rights of German Jews. The Nuremberg Laws of 1935 stripped them of citizenship, and later rules took away their jobs, their property, and their schools. Even then the aim of the regime was to force Jews out of Germany. But few countries were willing to take them in.

THE WANNSEE CONFERENCE

On January 20, 1942, fifteen high officials met in a lakeside villa in the Berlin suburb of Wannsee. Reinhard HeydrichRYN-hart HY-drikhReinhard Heydrich was the SS security chief who led the Wannsee Conference in January 1942. Read more below., the head of the SS security police, called the meeting. Adolf Eichmann, who handled Jewish matters for the SS, prepared it and wrote the minutes. The officials came from the ministries and the party, the police and the occupied lands. Heydrich wanted every office of the government to work together on what the Nazis called the “final solution of the Jewish question.”

Be careful about what the meeting was. It was not the moment the killing was decided, because mass murder was already under way. It was the moment when the officials of a modern state agreed to organize it. The minutes list about eleven million Jews, in countries from Ireland to Russia. They speak of deportationdee-por-TAY-shunThe forced removal of people from their homes to another country or region. See the glossary box. to the east, and of forced labor, in which a large part would “fall away.” Notice the plain, official words. The language hid the crime, and the clerks who wrote it were as necessary to it as the guards.

Over the next three years trains carried Jews from every country the Nazis held to camps built to kill, among them Belzec, Sobibor, Treblinka, and Auschwitz. About six million Jews were murdered, two of every three in Europe. Hundreds of thousands of Roma, disabled people, and others were also killed, and millions of Soviet prisoners and Polish civilians died. The guilt belongs to those who planned it and those who carried it out, and it began with Hitler’s hatred.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Reinhard Heydrich
RYN-hart HY-drikh

Reinhard Heydrich (1904 to 1942) was a former naval officer who joined the SS in 1931. Heinrich Himmler made him head of the security police, and he built the secret police into a machine of terror. He was cold, ambitious, and able.

In 1941 he was ordered to prepare a plan for dealing with the Jews of Europe, and in 1942 he chaired the Wannsee Conference. That year he was the German ruler of Bohemia and Moravia. In May 1942 Czech agents sent from London wounded him in Prague with a bomb, and he died of the wounds. The Nazis answered with terrible revenge on Czech villages.

WHAT THE ALLIES KNEW

The Allies learned of the killing in pieces. In 1941 British cryptanalysiskrip-tuh-NAL-ih-sisThe work of breaking an enemy’s secret codes so that its messages can be read. See the glossary box. read German police radio reports that counted the dead in the east, and on August 24 Churchill spoke of “scores of thousands of executions in cold blood.” In August 1942 a representative of the World Jewish Congress in Switzerland, Gerhart Riegner, sent a telegram that gave the plan for the first time. The State Department held it back for a time, but it was confirmed. On December 17, 1942, eleven Allied governments condemned the “bestial policy of cold-blooded extermination” in a joint declaration.

Other witnesses came forward. A Polish underground courier, Jan KarskiYAHN KAR-skeeJan Karski was a Polish underground courier who told Allied leaders what he had seen of the Nazi persecution of the Jews. Read more below., saw the Warsaw ghetto and told Allied leaders in London and Washington what he had seen. In April 1944 two prisoners, Rudolf Vrba and Alfred Wetzler, escaped from Auschwitz and wrote a report on how it worked. Newspapers in Britain and America printed the declaration, but many readers found the reports too terrible to believe, and some officials doubted them for the same reason. The question is no longer whether the Allies knew. They knew a great deal. The question is what they could have done about it.

👤 HISTORICAL FIGURE
Jan Karski
YAHN KAR-skee

Jan Karski (1914 to 2000) was a young Polish diplomat and army officer. After Germany and the Soviet Union divided Poland, he joined the Polish underground and carried messages across Europe. In 1942 Jewish leaders in Warsaw helped him enter the ghetto and a nearby camp, so that he could describe what he saw.

He made his way to London, and in July 1943 he told President Roosevelt what he had witnessed. Many officials found it hard to believe, or they did not know what to do. Karski kept speaking about it after the war, and he taught at Georgetown University for forty years. In 1982 Israel named him one of the Righteous Among the Nations.

WHAT COULD BE DONE

Historians discuss several choices. One was to admit more refugees. Before the war the Allies had held a conference at Evian, in 1938, and few nations offered to take in more. In 1939 the American ship St. Louis was turned away with hundreds of Jewish passengers, and Britain limited Jewish immigration to Palestine. Another choice was to bomb. In the summer of 1944, when the Hungarian Jews were being sent to Auschwitz, Jewish leaders asked the Allies to bomb the camp or the railways leading to it. The American War Department answered that it would take planes from the war effort and might provoke worse revenge. Bombers were in fact hitting a factory a few miles from the camp at that time.

Historians do not agree on whether bombing could have saved many lives. Rail lines were repaired quickly, and bombs would have killed prisoners. Others argue that the Allies should at least have tried. The Allied governments chose another path, which they called rescue through victory. They believed the fastest way to end the killing was to win, and the second frontSEK-und FRUNTA new theater of war opened against an enemy who is already fighting elsewhere, to divide its forces. See the glossary box. and the advance into Germany were meant to do that. Some people did act. In October 1943 Danes ferried almost all of Denmark’s Jews to Sweden. In 1944 the American War Refugee Board and diplomats such as Raoul Wallenberg helped to save Hungarian Jews.

FOR YOUR DISCUSSION POST

When you write about this subject, keep three questions apart. What did governments know, and when? What choices did they have, and what would each have cost? What did they decide? A fair answer needs evidence for each. Some people did their duty at great risk, and some did not. Write your notes in your notebook, and be ready to defend your judgment with the facts you have read.
